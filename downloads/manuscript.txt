Disclosure Regulation, Stock Liquidity, and
International Policy Diffusion
26 October 2026
Abstract
We study the capital-market consequences of public disclosure requirements and the forces
associated with their international spread. We combine official policy records with global
market data and cross-border portfolio holdings. Five public-disclosure families support a
common stacked comparison covering 30 country–family implementations in 46 countries.
Quoted spreads are approximately 20.0% lower under equal family objective mass and 18.6%
lower under market-cap objective mass in the three years after implementation relative to the
pre-reform comparison. Explicit family averages and covariance-weighted pooling also yield
negative spread estimates. We then distinguish foreign policy exposure, observed outcomes
among prior adopters, copying of larger economies and external financial dependence. The
implementation-hazard models show a positive interaction between portfolio exposure and
observed foreign emissions reductions. Institutional sources document the use of international
reporting templates. Together, the analyses connect the market consequences of disclosure
with the information and institutions through which reporting requirements travel.
Keywords:disclosure regulation; stock liquidity; policy diffusion; learning; international reporting.

I INTRODUCTION
Extensive literature documents how disclosure affects information asymmetry, investor participa-
tion and the cost of trading (Diamond and Verrecchia 1991; Welker 1995; Healy et al. 1999; Leuz
and Verrecchia 2000). International reporting studies connect these benefits to enforcement and re-
porting incentives (Daske et al. 2008; Christensen et al. 2013; Christensen et al. 2016). Research on
mandatory environmental, social and governance disclosure extends this evidence to nonfinancial
information (Krueger et al. 2024; Christensen et al. 2021). These studies provide a foundation for
understanding individual reporting reforms. There is a gap in our understanding of how liquidity
changes across disclosure requirements and how these requirements spread internationally.
We find that quoted spreads are lower around public-disclosure implementation across five
supported policy families. The stacked estimates imply approximately 20.0% lower spreads with
equal family objective mass and 18.6% with market-cap objective mass. Explicit equal-family
and market-cap averages are both about -0.199 in log units. Covariance-weighted pooling yields
-0.238. We then examine whether countries respond to foreign adoption alone or to the outcomes
observed among prior adopters. Portfolio exposure interacts positively with observed foreign emis-
sions reductions in the implementation-hazard models.
If disclosure rules improve transparency, reduce information asymmetry and support effi-
cient capital allocation, it would be reasonable to expect jurisdictions to adopt them promptly and
uniformly. In practice, the global pattern looks different. Adoption occurs at different times and
varies widely in scope. These differences raise an important question: what forces drive govern-
ments to adopt disclosure regulations? We study this question alongside the liquidity patterns that
give governments and market participants a reason to consider the rules.
We use the setting of international public-disclosure requirements because it combines a
common information channel with variation in the content, audience and timing of regulation. The
corpus identifies ten public-disclosure families, including financial reporting, auditor reporting,
ESG and climate reporting, public country reports and supply-chain disclosure. Five families sup-
port the same six-year liquidity window and documented later-adopter comparisons. This primary
1

sample contains 30 country–family implementations, 14 family–cohorts and 46 countries including
comparison countries. Confidential tax reporting forms a separate audience comparison.
We adjust for prior income, GDP growth and trade relative to GDP and continue to find
negative spread coefficients in the control-eligible stacked sample. The coefficients are -0.187 un-
der equal family mass and -0.183 under market-cap family mass. We also distinguish a directly
stacked slope from an explicit average of policy-family coefficients. This distinction makes clear
whether a result describes a common regression slope, the typical supported family or larger sam-
pled market exposures.
To account for alternative explanations, we examine other liquidity measures, the timing
of the outcome window and the treatment of extreme observations. We keep the same compa-
nies within each comparison window and use countries with documented implementation after the
window as controls. We compare untrimmed annual measures with 1% and 2.5% trimming and
estimate event-time coefficients. These tests show how the pattern depends on the trading measure,
sample support and policy clock.
We also distinguish foreign exposure from the conditions under which diffusion mecha-
nisms operate. Following Shipan and V olden (2008) and Koning et al. (2018), we examine learn-
ing, copying, competition and external pressure. Learning uses observed outcomes among prior
adopters; copying uses prior implementation by a fixed larger economy and language peers. We
compare independent national choices with documented coordinated implementations and include
family-specific international waves in the within-country models. This structure connects the dif-
fusion tests to the institutional source of adoption.
This paper contributes to several strands of literature. First, it contributes to research on
disclosure and capital markets by comparing different public reporting requirements within one
empirical framework. The financial-reporting literature establishes the importance of incentives
and enforcement, while nonfinancial reporting research examines information about environmen-
tal and social activities (Daske et al. 2008; Christensen et al. 2013; Krueger et al. 2024). Our
contribution is the common comparison across these requirements, using consistent market mea-
2

sures, company-selection rules and clearly defined pooling targets.
Second, the paper contributes to research on the international spread of accounting and fi-
nancial policies (Simmons and Elkins 2004; Simmons et al. 2006; Koning et al. 2018). When pol-
icy change is shaped by the prior choices of other jurisdictions, the process is commonly described
as policy diffusion. We distinguish broad financial exposure from observed foreign outcomes and
institutional templates. This distinction brings the market consequences of reporting requirements
into the study of their adoption, while preserving the separate meanings of an adoption association,
a policy effect and a diffusion mechanism.
II INSTITUTIONAL SETTING AND PREDICTIONS
Public information and capital markets
Disclosure regulations have meaningful implications for investment decisions and market func-
tioning. Public reports can reduce the cost of obtaining information and make companies easier
to compare. Better information can narrow the gap between investors with different information
and support trading (Diamond and Verrecchia 1991; Healy and Palepu 2001; Beyer et al. 2010).
The channel is common to financial statements, expanded auditor reports, climate disclosures and
public country reports, although the content differs across families.
The audience matters. Confidential country-by-country tax reports are filed with tax au-
thorities and may be exchanged between them. Public bank and multinational country reports
instead make specified jurisdictional information available to investors. Confidential reports can
change behaviour through tax enforcement; public reports also change the information directly
available to the market (De Simone and Olbert 2022; Müller et al. 2024; Nessa et al. 2025). We
therefore separate the public-disclosure pool from confidential reporting.
3

National choices and international experience
When a government adopts a new policy, the impetus can come from within the polity or from out-
side it. Governments may learn from the experiences of early adopters, copy prominent templates,
respond to economic rivals or face pressure from an international institution. These mechanisms
imply different reasons for responding to the same foreign policy (Shipan and V olden 2008; Kon-
ing et al. 2018). Learning requires information about outcomes. Copying can follow the visibility
or legitimacy of the adopter even when its outcomes are not yet observed.
Cross-border financial ties can expose investors and regulators to foreign reporting prac-
tices. Cross-listing may also make firms, auditors and exchanges familiar with those practices.
This experience can lower implementation costs, but it can also reduce the additional need for a
domestic requirement when firms already comply abroad. International standards and professional
implementation networks provide another route through which reporting templates become avail-
able across countries. Coordinated legal obligations, including supranational mandates, differ from
a national decision to copy a peer.
The tension and predictions
Useful public information should reduce trading frictions. The benefits depend on whether re-
ports add information that investors can use, how much they cost to process and how reporting
requirements are implemented (Blankespoor et al. 2020, 2014; Lennox et al. 2023). We therefore
predict lower illiquidity around public-disclosure implementation and examine whether the pattern
changes across market and institutional settings.
A market benefit alone need not produce immediate adoption. Governments weigh imple-
mentation costs, domestic priorities and the credibility of foreign experience. Learning predicts a
stronger response to foreign exposure when prior adopters have visible improvements. Copying
predicts a response to a larger or related adopter beyond the outcome signal. Competition and
external finance provide parallel explanations for national choices. These predictions guide the
measures and comparison models; the regressions describe observable associations through which
4

the proposed mechanisms can be assessed.
III DA TA AND EMPIRICAL DESIGN
Primary policy data
Our primary policy data are a register of official laws, standards and implementation materials.
These sources are public and verifiable. Each record identifies a country, disclosure family, au-
dience, scope and the basis for its date. Treatment measures country-market exposure to a doc-
umented rule; issuer, sector, size and jurisdictional coverage are recorded separately. The wider
register contains 482 documented country–family histories, including 60 records identifying coun-
tries outside the direct home-country geography of EU bank reporting. These geographic controls
are excluded from the implementation-hazard risk sets. The ten-family public corpus is reported
separately from confidential tax reporting and broader institutional regulation.
Announcement, legal application, the first covered financial period, the filing deadline and
publication can occur in different years. We use the reviewed implementation or reporting-phase
date stated in the source record. The resulting clock concerns the documented phase and does
not assume an observed filing date for every issuer. Unknown histories remain missing. The
implementation-hazard panel stops at the first recorded implementation, rather than carrying post-
implementation years into the first-event risk set.
Market and country data
The secondary market data are licensed Datastream observations during 2000–2025. The inventory
contains 1,055,142 stock-years, 99,100 vendor company records and 90 jurisdictions. For each
liquidity cohort, we select the company’s largest positive-market-value share line in yearg−1 and
hold the company, share line and country fixed over the full event window. At least 30 companies
with all three outcomes must remain in each country–stack. The primary stacked sample contains
1,002 country–stack–years in 46 countries (Table 1).
5

We measure annual illiquidity using log quoted spread, log price impact and the fraction
of observed daily returns equal to zero. Spread is the annual median relative bid–ask spread. Price
impact is the annual median absolute return divided by dollar trading volume, adapting Amihud
(2002). The annual screening requires 120 valid observations for the relevant measure. Positive
spread and impact values are trimmed within year at the 1st and 99th percentiles before logging.
We also report an equally weighted standardized index of the three measures. Lower values indi-
cate greater liquidity. Online Appendix Table A1 gives the complete construction and denomina-
tors.
The IMF Portfolio Investment Positions dataset provides observed bilateral asset positions.
We select annual total portfolio assets for the total economy and total-economy counterpart, con-
vert reported scales to US dollars and exclude self positions. World Bank data provide income,
GDP growth, trade and external finance. Geographic and official-language networks define peers.
Reported UNFCCC emissions excluding land use, land-use change and forestry provide an ob-
served environmental-outcome signal. The cross-listing measure is the preceding year’s fraction
of sampled firms listed abroad. We add no interpolation or forward/backward filling to the liquidity
or emissions success measures.

IV LIQUIDITY FINDINGS
The collective spread comparison
The institutional analysis identifies a common information channel across otherwise different re-
porting requirements. We first ask whether this channel is reflected in trading costs around im-
plementation. We regress country–stack–year log spread on the adopting-country post indicator
using the fixed effects and weights described above. Table 3 presents the five pooling models on
the same country and company support.
The stacked coefficient is -0.223 with equal family objective mass and -0.206 with market-
cap objective mass. These values correspond to approximately 20.0% and 18.6% lower quoted
spreads. The explicit equal-family and market-cap averages are -0.199 and -0.199, respectively.
8

GLS gives -0.238. Each supported family’s spread coefficient is also negative in this common
window. Figure 1 shows the pooled magnitudes and conditional intervals. The negative coeffi-
cients under all five pooling methods provide a consistent directional association between public
disclosure and lower trading costs in the supported sample.
Country conditions and alternative trading measures
The common sample comparison leaves open whether changing economic conditions account for
the spread pattern. We therefore add prior income, GDP growth and trade/GDP. The stacked co-
efficients remain negative at -0.187 and -0.183 on the control-complete sample (Online Appendix
Table A2). The explicit family averages are -0.147 and -0.160. This shows that the negative spread
pattern also appears after accounting for the included country conditions.
Spread measures the cost of immediately trading against quoted prices. Price impact and
zero-return share address different aspects of trading. Online Appendix Table A3 reports all four
outcomes on the same six-year sample, including the standardized index. The alternative measures
add information about how trading changes across policy families and pooling targets. We retain
each outcome so the spread result can be assessed alongside the wider trading evidence.
Timing, outliers and sample support
The negative spread estimates make timing and sample composition important next questions. We
therefore vary annual trimming, the first post-policy year and the length of the comparison win-
dow. Online Appendix Tables A4–A5 report the event-time coefficients and every stated stacked
alternative. Secondary country–cohort tests examine prior liquidity, regulatory quality, market de-
velopment, income, trade and matched comparison countries (Tables A8–A9). These analyses
separate the six-year primary comparison from tests that need different data support.
The two stacked coefficients remain negative under all three trimming rules in the bench-
mark years 1–3 window. Table 2 shows how adopting-country coverage and effective weights
differ across families. Extended Appendix Table E4 then imposes minimum adopting-country

support. Taken together, these comparisons show which parts of the spread pattern recur across
constructions and which depend on the policy sample.
V INTERNA TIONAL POLICY DIFFUSION
Foreign exposure and the implementation decision
The liquidity analysis identifies a market pattern that could make disclosure attractive. It leaves
unanswered why countries implement requirements at different times. Foreign exposure provides
a first way to examine this question: it measures contact with markets that already operate under
the rules. We estimate the implementation-hazard model using prior portfolio exposure and the
country controls. Columns (1)–(2) of Table 4 report the results.
The exposure coefficients are -0.422 under equal family weights and -0.676 under cap
weights, with standard errors of 0.803 and 0.950. The estimates concern financial connectedness
to documented adopters. They motivate distinguishing exposure itself from the information carried
by that exposure. The next models ask whether observed foreign outcomes change the relationship
between exposure and implementation.
Learning from observed outcomes
Exposure can bring governments into contact with a reporting regime without showing whether
the regime works. Following the success-based distinction in Koning et al. (2018), we combine
exposure with observed outcomes among prior adopters. Liquidity and emissions address parallel
questions: whether adoption is associated with visible market improvements or with environmental
improvements. The models include exposure, the corresponding success share and their interac-
tion.
For liquidity success, the interaction coefficients are 1.835 and 2.616 under equal and cap
family weights (Table 4, columns (3)–(4)). The corresponding standard errors are 1.247 and 1.446.
The positive interaction means that the portfolio-exposure slope becomes more positive as the
10

observed foreign liquidity-success share rises. This connects the liquidity analysis to a conditional
association in the implementation model.
The environmental interaction is 2.140 under equal weights and 2.351 under cap weights,
with standard errors of 0.830 and 0.787 (columns (5)–(6)). Both interaction coefficients are pos-
itive and conventionally significant. Countries with greater exposure to prior adopters reporting
emissions reductions have a more positive exposure–implementation relationship in these hazard
specifications. This provides evidence of a conditional association between observed foreign out-
comes and national implementation.
The conditional models leave a further question about domestic relevance. A foreign out-
come may matter differently depending on the focal country’s market or emissions position. Online
Appendix Table A7 therefore includes domestic capacity, the two foreign-signal primitives and ev-
ery lower-order interaction. Table A6 varies portfolio timing and history coverage. These tests
make the content and availability of the foreign signal explicit.
Copying, competition and international waves
Observed success is one reason to follow another jurisdiction; the prominence of the adopter is
another. We therefore examine the fixed nearest larger economy and separate its recent from older
implementation. The available independent-choice sample contains 12 countries, below the stated
support requirement. Online Appendix Table A10 reports that coverage and the all-documented
comparison. Institutional sources provide a complementary way to observe the availability of
templates: the XRB describes New Zealand Climate Standards as based on TCFD, while IAASB
and IFAC materials document international auditor-reporting standards and their dissemination
(Table 6). These sources show concrete routes through which reporting practices become available
across jurisdictions.
Copying, competition and external pressure address parallel explanations for adoption.
Table 5 reports within-country implementation models for GDP-growth learning, language-peer
adoption, external finance and cross-listing familiarity. Each includes family-specific international
11

waves. The equal-weight coefficients are -0.045, 0.058, -0.002 and 0.000, respectively; the full
estimates, standard errors and paired cap weights are in the table. These comparisons place the
foreign-outcome hazard estimates alongside persistent national conditions and common policy-
family timing.
The all-documented trade-competition logit gives negative coefficients of -1.292 and -1.834
across the two weights, with the complete results in the accompanying matrix. This differs from a
general prediction that countries follow their export competitors. The distinction between indepen-
dent choices and coordinated obligations is therefore central to interpreting the observed adoption
pattern.
VI CONCLUSION
We study public-disclosure requirements in two parts: their liquidity patterns and their interna-
tional implementation. The five retained pooling models all produce negative quoted-spread co-
efficients on the same supported sample. The two directly stacked estimates imply spreads ap-
proximately 20.0% and 18.6% lower around implementation under the stated family objective
masses. The diffusion analysis separates exposure to foreign rules from observed outcomes among
prior adopters and the availability of reporting templates. It shows a positive exposure–emissions-
reduction interaction in the implementation-hazard specifications.
The evidence also identifies the conditions that matter for interpretation. Spread differences
are present before implementation in the event analysis. Two supported liquidity families have
only one adopting country, and GLS assigns 59.6% of its benchmark weight to climate disclosure.
The hazard interaction does not survive the full diffusion-matrix adjustment, and complementary
within-country specifications give different estimates. We address these issues by holding com-
panies fixed, using documented comparison histories, reporting event leads, varying timing and
weights, and retaining support restrictions and complete model matrices. Future work can sharpen
causal interpretation with more complete earliest-law histories, observed issuer filing dates and
direct evidence of policymakers’ use of foreign outcomes.
