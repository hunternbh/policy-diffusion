Disclosure Regulation, Stock Liquidity, and
International Policy Diffusion
26 October 2026
Abstract
We study stock-market liquidity around disclosure regulation and the international adoption
of these requirements. Our data cover 90 jurisdictions during 2000–2025. Public disclosures
form the main analysis; confidential tax reporting is examined separately. Following-year es-
timates imply 2.2% and 4.5% lower spreads under equal and market-cap family weights, but
both confidence intervals include zero. The other liquidity measures and pre-policy patterns
do not establish a common causal improvement. Adoption is positively associated with prior
language-peer adoption under common year controls; this association changes when we ac-
count for family-specific international waves. The study compares disclosure requirements
using consistent outcomes, comparison rules and weighting.
Keywords:disclosure regulation; stock liquidity; international reporting; policy diffusion.
Research working paper

I INTRODUCTION AND RELATED RESEARCH
Disclosure rules aim to improve the information available to investors. Better information can
make companies easier to compare and reduce the cost of trading. We ask whether these benefits
extend across different disclosure requirements, and how the requirements spread internationally.
The paper has two parts: liquidity around implementation, followed by international policy adop-
tion.
The link between disclosure and liquidity is well established. Diamond and Verrecchia
(1991) explain how public information affects information asymmetry and investor participa-
tion. Early studies document associations between disclosure and trading outcomes (Welker 1995;
Healy et al. 1999; Leuz and Verrecchia 2000). Reviews emphasize that mandated reports, volun-
tary disclosure and information intermediaries jointly shape the information available to investors
(Healy and Palepu 2001; Beyer et al. 2010; Leuz and Wysocki 2016). A new rule can therefore
add information, reduce the cost of obtaining it, or repeat what investors already know.
International evidence shows that the setting matters. IFRS studies relate liquidity benefits
to reporting incentives and enforcement (Daske et al. 2008; Christensen et al. 2013). Securities-
law studies distinguish the rule itself from implementation, liability and enforcement (Christensen
et al. 2016; La Porta et al. 2006; Bhattacharya and Daouk 2002). In nonfinancial reporting, Krueger
et al. (2024) examine mandatory E, S and G disclosure globally, while Christensen et al. (2021)
review its wider economic consequences. Processing costs and limited new information can also
constrain the benefits of public reports (Blankespoor et al. 2020, 2014; Lennox et al. 2023).
The audience for disclosure is another important distinction. Confidential tax reports can
change company decisions through tax enforcement, while public reports also reach investors
(De Simone and Olbert 2022; Müller et al. 2024; Nessa et al. 2025). Payments-to-government re-
quirements target corporate conduct as well as information provision (Rauter 2020). We therefore
make public disclosure the main liquidity analysis and examine confidential reporting separately.
Our contribution is a common comparison across a broader set of documented disclosure
families. Existing studies establish effects for particular reforms; we ask how the average changes
1

when different requirements are evaluated using the same liquidity measures, comparison rules
and weights. The data cover ten identified public-disclosure families, rather than every disclosure
obligation ever enacted. Eight families support following-year comparisons and six support years
1–3.
Spread estimates are negative under both weighting schemes. The following-year estimates
imply approximately 2.2% lower spreads under equal family weights and 4.5% under market-cap
weights, although both intervals include zero. Other outcomes and pre-policy differences limit a
general causal interpretation. We then examine competition, learning, emulation and external fi-
nance as explanations for adoption, following Simmons and Elkins (2004); Simmons et al. (2006);
Koning et al. (2018). The strongest common-year language-peer association changes when family-
specific adoption waves are absorbed.
II INSTITUTIONAL SETTING AND PREDICTIONS
The public-disclosure sample covers financial reporting, auditor reporting, nonfinancial and cli-
mate reporting, public country reports, payments to governments, supply-chain reporting, sustain-
ability reporting and electronic financial reporting. National implementations and later phases can
differ within a family. Broader governance, audit-institution, ownership-register and digital-asset
categories remain in the wider 17-family analysis because they do not consistently identify a new
public reporting obligation for the sampled listed companies. Table 2 reports the inventory and
estimation coverage.
Confidential country-by-country reports are filed with tax authorities and can be exchanged
between them. Public bank and multinational country reports instead make specified jurisdictional
information publicly available. Both can matter for capital markets, but through different channels.
Comparisons across these families also reflect differences in coverage, countries and timing, so
they do not isolate the effect of publication alone.
Timing is equally important. Announcement, legal application, the first covered financial
period, the filing deadline and actual publication can fall in different years. For UK extended
2

audit reporting, we use the 2013 information-policy year and retain the 2012 covered-period start.
The broader nonfinancial calendar often records later phases than the supplied comprehensive E,
S and G calendar; a separate benchmark uses that calendar through 2020. The global register does
not contain actual filing dates for every company. Flagged 2025 reporting changes in the US and
Germany are censored where relevant, rather than recoded as nonadoption.
Useful public information should reduce trading frictions. Benefits may be smaller when
reports duplicate existing information, are costly to process, or are weakly enforced. We examine
prior liquidity, regulatory quality, financial development, income and openness as possible sources
of variation. These country characteristics are proxies: regulatory quality does not measure en-
forcement of a particular rule, and prior illiquidity can also capture mean reversion.
III DATA AND EMPIRICAL DESIGN
Sample and outcomes
Datastream provides 1,055,142 stock-year observations for 99,100 vendor company records in 90
jurisdictions during 2000–2025. All three liquidity measures are jointly available for 636,960
stock-years in 86 jurisdictions (Table 1). For each implementation cohort, we select the company’s
largest positive-market-value share line in the preceding year and keep the company, share line and
country fixed throughout the comparison window. Country averages require at least 30 companies
with complete outcomes. Complete future observations select the sample, and the resulting country
exposure is not verified legal coverage of every issuer.
The wider source register contains 482 documented country-family phase histories. Con-
trols must have a documented implementation date after the outcome window. Unknown histories
are missing, not nonadoption; already-treated countries are excluded. The register is not a com-
plete history of earliest laws and nonadoption, which narrows the comparison sample. Country data
provide GDP, income, growth, trade, regulatory quality and market capitalisation relative to GDP.
Missing values are not replaced by zeros, and macroeconomic series are contemporary revisions
rather than historical information vintages.
3

We use three measures of illiquidity: log quoted spread, log price impact and the share of
observed daily returns equal to zero. Spread is the annual median relative quoted bid-ask spread.
Price impact is the annual median absolute daily return divided by dollar volume, adapting Amihud
(2002); the median differs from the original annual mean. Zero-return share captures infrequent
price changes rather than the structural transaction-cost measure of Lesmond et al. (1999). Annual
measures apply valid-day thresholds, including 120 valid observations for the relevant daily mea-
sure. Log spread and impact are winsorised within year at the first and ninety-ninth percentiles.
Lower values indicate greater liquidity, although the measures capture different trading frictions.

Country regressions use positive prior-year GDP weights. Within each family, cohort coef-
ficients are weighted by the GDP of supported adopting countries. Across families, equal weights
describe the typical supported family; market-cap weights emphasize larger sampled adopting-
company exposures, measured ing−1. A company can enter several families, so this is not a
portfolio of distinct world companies. Both weighting schemes use the same family estimates.
Separating cohort comparisons from aggregation follows the logic of Callaway and
Sant’Anna (2021) and avoids comparisons with already-treated countries (Goodman-Bacon
2021; Sun and Abraham 2021). This country-cohort WLS adaptation still requires credible
counterfactual trends; it does not resolve endogenous adoption or overlapping reforms (Baker
4

et al. 2022; Roth et al. 2023). Country influence contributions are combined across cohorts
and families before calculating uncertainty. Inference is conditional on recorded dates, sample
membership and, where applicable, selected matches; coordinated adoption and legal-date error
remain concerns.
Controls add prior income, GDP growth and trade/GDP on identical samples. Cross sec-
tions interact treatment with a prior country characteristic, retaining its main effect and standard-
ising it within cohort. These fits require two adopting and six comparison countries, full rank and
three residual degrees of freedom. Matching selects three nearest documented controls per adopter
using prior income, trade and all three liquidity levels; controls can be reused. The event analysis
holds companies fixed from three years before to three years after implementation.
These extensions were specified after earlier results were known. We retain the wider
17-family analysis and its 1,296-specification grid. Holm (1979) adjustments cover 12 primary
liquidity coefficients, 108 additional WLS and audience coefficients, and 64 within-country dif-
fusion coefficient attempts. Adjustment addresses multiple testing, not model misspecification or
retrospective design choices.
IV LIQUIDITY RESULTS
The overall comparison
Table 3 reports following-year log-spread estimates of -0.022 under equal family weights and -
0.047 under market-cap weights: approximately 2.2% and 4.5% lower spreads. Both confidence
intervals include zero. Longer-window estimates are -0.075 and -0.084, but cover six families
rather than eight and cannot be interpreted as the time path of the same sample. Price-impact
estimates vary with the weight and horizon; several zero-return estimates are negative. None of the
12 primary coefficients survives the stated multiple-test adjustment.
Family results show what enters the average (Table 4 and Figure 1). Nonfinancial reporting
has a log-spread estimate of about -0.065 across 22 adopting jurisdictions, or roughly 6% lower
spreads. Other family estimates differ in direction and precision. Because their countries and
5

implementation cohorts differ, these comparisons reflect both policy design and the settings in
which adoption occurs.
Confidential tax reporting changes the composition and meaning of the pooled estimate.
Table 3 therefore shows the combined public-and-confidential pool alongside the public pool. Ap-
pendix Table A2 reports the audience groups and a formal contrast accounting for shared country
influence. The equal-family public-minus-confidential spread contrast is -0.121; it does not survive
the extension-wide adjustment and cannot isolate the causal effect of making the same information
public.
Controls, cross sections and dynamics
On the five-family control-eligible sample, the equal-family spread estimate changes from -0.063
without controls to -0.040 with controls (Appendix Table A1). Appendix Tables A3–A6 examine
prior liquidity, regulatory quality, financial development, income and openness. Each spread inter-
action rests on only two supported public families, and none survives the 108-test correction. This
limited support prevents a general conclusion about where disclosure improves liquidity most.
For the four-family matching sample, the equal-family spread estimate is -0.073 before
matching and -0.126 after matching (Appendix Table A7). The matched interval includes zero and
material covariate imbalance remains. The more negative coefficient therefore does not establish a
better counterfactual. Matching uncertainty is conditional on the chosen controls.
Appendix Table A8 and Figure 2 show five families on a fixed seven-year company sam-
ple, with year -1 as the reference. Spread differences are already visible before implementation.
Anticipation, endogenous policy timing or other market changes can therefore contribute to the
later decline. Pre-policy tests diagnose this problem but do not establish acceptable counterfac-
tual trends (Rambachan and Roth 2023). The spread point estimates consistently point downward
across the two main weights and horizons; the broader evidence does not yet establish a common
causal liquidity improvement.
6

Stacked comparisons and collective effects
We also adapt the stacked design of Krueger et al. (2024), using country-by-cohort and year-by-
cohort effects. With GDP country weights, three pre-policy years and years 1–3 after adoption, the
direct stacked spread coefficients are -0.223 and -0.206 under equal and market-cap family mass.
Both reject the pre-policy lead test. Covariance-weighted pooling gives a more precise estimate
of -0.238, but assigns 59.6% to climate disclosure supported by one adopting country. Requir-
ing five adopters per family leaves two families and imprecise negative estimates. Extended Ap-
pendix Tables E4–E5 and the accompanying HTML retain the full 5,184-specification grid and 576
adoption-support diagnostics. These results support investigating lower spreads, but the weighting
and pre-policy patterns prevent a general causal conclusion.
V INTERNATIONAL POLICY DIFFUSION
Adoption and foreign signals
The second part follows Koning et al. (2018) in examining competition, learning, emulation and
external finance. We distinguish whether a documented policy is in place from first adoption
while the country remains at risk. The outcomes describe an established regime and a transition,
respectively.
Competition uses lagged adoption among five countries with similar export-market pat-
terns, with the main network fixed using 2000 trade data. Learning compares lagged GDP growth
among ten geographic peers with documented histories. The indicator is zero when none has
adopted; with partial adoption it compares adopter and nonadopter medians; with full adoption it
compares adopter growth with the focal country’s growth. Language-peer adoption proxies for em-
ulation. External finance combines aid and IMF credit relative to GDP. Unknown peer histories are
not coded as nonadoption. Language proximity differs from the cultural-distance measure in the
JIBS paper, and lagged foreign signals can still reflect shared shocks or common legal obligations.
7

Results
Appendix Table A9 records the reference-style logit models with family and year effects. The joint
public-sample model has no usable observations after common-sample and outcome-variation re-
strictions. Separate models have insufficient country support or unstable fits. These are estimation
limitations, not estimates of zero diffusion.
Table 5 and Appendix Table A10 use weighted linear probability models with country-
family effects, which remove persistent differences in national policy regimes. We compare com-
mon year effects with family-year effects that absorb international waves specific to each disclosure
family. Both weights use identical observations within each comparison. The family-wave sensi-
tivity was added after initial within-country results were seen; all 64 coefficient attempts remain in
the adjustment.
With common year effects, a ten-percentage-point increase in prior language-peer adop-
tion is associated with a 6.6-point higher probability that a policy is in place under equal family
weights. The estimate covers 45 countries and eight families and survives the stated adjustment.
With family-year effects, it changes to -8.4 points and its adjustedp-value is 0.764. Competition,
learning and external finance do not provide consistent adjusted evidence in the separate equal-
family status models. First-adoption estimates and both weights remain in the tables.
The language comparison shows why common policy waves matter: countries can adopt
together without one learning from or imitating another. The current results describe conditional
adoption associations. More complete historical risk sets and stronger separation of coordinated
mandates from peer transmission are needed to identify diffusion mechanisms.
VI CONCLUSION
We compare liquidity around different disclosure requirements and then examine their interna-
tional adoption. Public-disclosure spread estimates are negative under both family weights, but
uncertainty, other outcomes and pre-policy differences prevent a general causal conclusion. The
positive language-peer adoption association is sensitive to family-specific international waves. The
8

main contribution is a common comparison across reporting requirements with different audiences
and implementation histories. Completing those histories and identifying when covered companies
actually release new information are the next steps toward stronger conclusions.
Supporting results are in the Online Appendix. The Extended Appendix retains the broader regu-
latory comparisons and specification audit.
