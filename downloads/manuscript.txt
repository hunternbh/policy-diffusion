Disclosure Regulation, Stock Liquidity, and
International Policy Diffusion
31 October 2026
Abstract
We examine stock liquidity around public-disclosure requirements and the international spread
of these requirements. The policy calendar contains 211 country–family implementation mile-
stones in 65 countries during 2000–2025. A common three-year comparison supports five fam-
ilies, 61 implementations and 47 countries. The equal-family stacked estimate corresponds to
17.3% lower trading costs after implementation. Market-cap weighting gives a smaller, less
precise estimate of 13.1%. We then distinguish exposure to foreign rules, observed outcomes
among earlier adopters, copying, competition and external financial dependence. Foreign-
policy exposure has a more positive association with implementation when earlier adopters
experience lower trading costs in the implementation-hazard models. This interaction is not
confirmed by the within-country specifications or the full multiple-testing adjustment. The
findings support a comparison of liquidity patterns across reporting requirements while identi-
fying the limits of a common liquidity effect and a learning interpretation.
Keywords:disclosure regulation; stock liquidity; policy diffusion; international reporting.

I INTRODUCTION
Governments increasingly require companies to publish information beyond their annual finan-
cial statements. Expanded auditor reports, environmental and social reports, country reports and
supply-chain statements address different information needs. We ask two related questions. Do
these public-disclosure requirements improve stock liquidity on average? What explains their in-
ternational implementation?
Our primary country-level comparison covers 61 country–family implementations in 47
countries. Trading costs are 17.3% lower after implementation under equal family weights.
Weighting families by the market value represented in their adopting-country samples gives an
estimated reduction of 13.1%, with a confidence interval that includes zero. The comparison
therefore provides evidence of lower trading costs under an equal-family target, rather than a
precise common benefit under every weighting scheme. Expanded auditor reporting contributes
adopting countries across several regions. The supported climate comparison remains much
narrower.
The diffusion tests relate implementation to information available from other countries.
In the hazard models, the interaction between portfolio exposure to foreign requirements and ob-
served liquidity improvements among earlier adopters is positive under both family weights. This
pattern is consistent with countries responding to favourable foreign experience. It remains ex-
ploratory: the corresponding within-country interaction is not positive, and the hazard interaction
does not survive the adjustment across all focal diffusion tests. We distinguish this conditional
pattern from unconditional exposure, copying of larger economies, competition and financial de-
pendence.
Disclosure research provides the basis for these questions. Information can reduce in-
vestors’ trading costs by limiting differences in information and improving the precision and com-
parability of public reports (Diamond and Verrecchia 1991; Welker 1995; Healy et al. 1999; Leuz
and Verrecchia 2000; Healy and Palepu 2001; Beyer et al. 2010). The benefits of mandatory
reporting depend on reporting incentives, enforcement and the costs of producing and using in-
1

formation (Daske et al. 2008; Christensen et al. 2013; Christensen et al. 2016; Leuz and Wysocki
2016; Blankespoor et al. 2020). Thus, a requirement to report need not produce the same market
response across countries or reporting topics.
An expanding literature examines particular reporting requirements. Research on sustain-
ability reporting considers environmental and social information, company responses and capital-
market outcomes (Christensen et al. 2021; Krueger et al. 2024). Other studies examine expanded
auditor reports, electronic reporting, extractive payments and country-by-country tax information
(Lennox et al. 2023; Blankespoor et al. 2014; Rauter 2020; De Simone and Olbert 2022; Müller
et al. 2024; Nessa et al. 2025). These studies motivate a common comparison across reporting
families. We bring the requirements into one framework while retaining their different audiences,
legal scopes and implementation dates. The analysis asks about the average pattern across sup-
ported requirements; it does not assume that all disclosure rules are equivalent.
The second question connects capital-market research to policy diffusion. Governments
can learn from outcomes abroad, follow a visible example, respond to competitors or face external
pressure (Simmons and Elkins 2004; Simmons et al. 2006; Shipan and V olden 2008). In account-
ing, Koning et al. (2018) distinguish these explanations for international IFRS adoption. We apply
that logic to a broader disclosure calendar and separate foreign policy exposure from observed for-
eign liquidity outcomes. This distinction allows a favourable outcome abroad to enter separately
from the mere existence of a foreign rule.
Our contribution is a common country-level comparison and a source-linked implementa-
tion calendar. The legal archive and the market-data universe are global; usable regression samples
depend on timing, market coverage and the availability of documented comparisons. We preserve
this distinction throughout. The paper first presents liquidity estimates and then examines interna-
tional implementation.

II INSTITUTIONAL SETTING AND PREDICTIONS
What information reaches investors?
The public-disclosure families cover financial statements under IFRS, expanded auditor reports,
environmental and social reporting, climate reporting, public bank and multinational country re-
ports, extractive payments, supply chains, sustainability-related financial reporting and electronic
financial reporting. These are international families of requirements implemented through national
laws, listing rules and standards. A country name identifies the jurisdiction and timing of imple-
mentation, rather than a separate country-specific research topic.
The information differs substantially. Financial-reporting reforms change the basis on
which reported numbers are prepared. Expanded auditor reports explain matters such as signif-
icant audit risks and key or critical audit matters. Environmental and social requirements can man-
date a report while allowing materiality judgements or explanations for particular omitted items.
We therefore distinguish a mandatory report from a mandate for every environmental, social and
governance metric. Optional guidance and recommended reporting frameworks do not become
mandatory simply because an exchange publishes them.
Country-by-country reporting also has distinct audiences. Public bank reports disclose
specified country-level activities and financial information. Public multinational reports provide
country-level tax information under separate eligibility and publication rules. Confidential tax re-
ports are transmitted to tax authorities and are not, by that obligation alone, available to investors.
Their liquidity consequences may reflect tax enforcement or other channels. We report them sepa-
rately from the public-disclosure headline estimate.
More useful public information can make investors less reluctant to trade against better-
informed counterparties. The expected result is lower trading costs and greater liquidity. Prepa-
ration costs, limited enforcement, boilerplate reporting, existing information and partial issuer
coverage can weaken that response. A country-level estimate also includes firms outside the legal
perimeter and possible spillovers. It measures a market-level response to a requirement, not an
3

effect on every covered issuer.
Why might a reporting requirement spread?
Learning requires information about outcomes. A country exposed to an earlier adopter may be-
come more willing to implement a requirement when that adopter experiences a favourable market
outcome. Exposure alone does not establish learning: foreign requirements may be visible without
producing an observed benefit.
Copying instead concerns the visibility or legitimacy of an example. A larger nearby econ-
omy or countries sharing a language may provide a familiar reporting template. Competition con-
cerns the possible cost of lagging behind an economic rival. External pressure concerns institutions
or financing relationships that influence domestic choices. Coordinated legal obligations, including
EU requirements, differ from independent national decisions. We separate those implementations
when examining the channels proposed by Koning et al. (2018).
III DATA AND EMPIRICAL DESIGN
Sample and datasets
The research universe contains 90 Datastream jurisdictions and market data for 2000–2025. Se-
lected source-reviewed legal provisions are available for 81 of those jurisdictions. We retain
statutes, standards, official notices, amendments and successor references, with document hashes
and links between the sources and coded facts. Scanned passages used in the review are checked
with OCR and page images. The archive records uncertainty rather than treating an unresolved
date or scope as nonadoption.
The empirical public-disclosure calendar contains 211 dated country–family milestones in
65 countries during 2000–2025 (Table 1 and Figure 1). It records implementation phases rather
than an exhaustive census of first-ever national disclosure laws. The full adoption risk panel,
including the separate confidential-tax comparison, covers 82 countries; the public-reporting risk
4

panel covers 66. Regression samples are smaller where a particular channel or market measure is
unavailable.
We distinguish legal adoption, entry into force, the first covered financial period, an en-
gagement boundary and a statutory filing deadline. The calendar preserves the clock supported by
the source. For example, China’s initial cross-listed audit phase differs from its later broader phase;
Canada’s mandatory TSX phase differs from the earlier general standard; and Dutch expanded re-
porting precedes the later EU reporting milestone. Vietnam’s greenhouse-gas addition applies after
the grandfathered 2020 annual report, whose requirements remain under the predecessor circular.
The Moroccan first implementation deadline is a filing obligation, not an observed issuer receipt.
We do not invent actual filing dates or require complete national histories for an otherwise sup-
ported implementation milestone. The evidence supplement lists the reconciled cases and their
coverage.
The market data provide three liquidity proxies following the reporting literature (Amihud
2002; Lesmond et al. 1999; Krueger et al. 2024).Bid-Ask Spreadmeasures the cost of trading
immediately; lower values indicate greater liquidity.Price Impactmeasures the association be-
tween absolute returns and dollar trading volume.Zero Returnsis the fraction of observed trading
days without a price change. We use log trading costs and log price impact, screen for sufficient
annual observations, and trim the first two measures at the annual first and ninety-ninth percentiles.
An additional illiquidity index averages the three standardized measures. Higher values of each
outcome indicate less liquidity.
World Bank data provide income, GDP growth, trade, aid and IMF credit measures. IMF
bilateral portfolio positions measure financial exposure to foreign markets. Fixed geographic,
language and export-pattern networks identify other peer groups. These public country data com-
plement the licensed market observations; the firm-level Datastream files are not publicly redis-
tributed.

Liquidity comparisons and weights
We form a separate comparison for each supported family and implementation cohort. For a mile-
stone in yearg, the primary window contains yearsg−3 throughg−1 andg+1 throughg+3. We
omit yeargas a transition year. Comparison countries have a documented later milestone after the
outcome window. An unknown date does not supply an untreated observation. This construction
avoids using already implemented cases as later-adopter controls for the same series, while recog-
nising that countries may have other or earlier reporting requirements (Goodman-Bacon 2021;
Callaway and Sant’Anna 2021; Sun and Abraham 2021; Baker et al. 2022).
We choose each company’s largest positive-market-value security line in yearg−1 and
hold the companies fixed over the window. A country must supply at least 30 qualifying compa-
nies with the required annual market measures. This gives a comparison among firms with persis-
tent observable trading data and limits changes in company composition. It also reduces country
coverage and can favour larger, more actively traded companies. Two-year windows assess this
restriction with a different horizon; they do not provide an identical sample.
Within each cohort we estimate a country- and year-effect comparison, with country
weights based on GDP ing−1. Cohort weights within a family reflect the baseline GDP
represented by adopting countries. The primary sample contains 1,404 country–stack–years, 47
countries, 61 country–family implementations and 18 family–cohorts. Country-cluster scores
retain dependence when a country appears in several cohorts or families.
We report two family weights and two aggregation methods. Equal family weights give
each supported family the same objective mass. Market-cap family weights use the baseline sam-
ple market value represented by adopting-country companies. They measure market value in the
estimation sample, not a complete census of legally covered issuers. A stacked common-coefficient
regression and an explicit weighted average of separately estimated family coefficients are differ-
ent targets. In the stacked regression, treatment variation also affects each family’s coefficient
contribution. We retain both methods rather than equating their labels. No weights are selected to
obtain a preferred sign or significance level.
6

The secondary specifications add lagged log income per capita, GDP growth and
trade/GDP; use two-year windows; and examine alternative liquidity proxies. Event-time coeffi-
cients show differences before and after implementation. These checks assess the comparison, but
cannot establish parallel trends or remove all coincident reforms (Roth et al. 2023; Rambachan
and Roth 2023).
International implementation tests
We estimate implementation-hazard models with family and year effects, and linear probability
models with country–family and family–year effects. The latter absorb persistent country-family
differences and family-specific international waves. Independent national choices form the main
comparison; coordinated implementations enter a separate comparison. All signals use the pre-
ceding year or earlier. These models explain documented implementation timing. Because the
political decision can precede implementation, they do not establish that governments used a sig-
nal before making their original decision.
Portfolio exposure is the share of observed foreign portfolio positions in countries that
have implemented the requirement. We require documented histories to cover at least half of
the observed portfolio mass. The liquidity-success signal measures lower trading costs among
the same companies in earlier adopting countries. A donor must have implemented byt−2; its
observed change ends int−1. This is an observed foreign outcome, not an estimated liquidity
improvement caused by the foreign policy. We include exposure, success and their interaction
together.
Following Koning et al. (2018), growth learning compares prior-year growth among earlier
and later adopters in a geographic network with documented histories. Copying uses a larger
geographic peer fixed with GDP in 2000, distinguishing recent and older implementation; language
peers provide a second proxy. Competition uses export-pattern peers selected in 2000. Financial
dependence adds aid to IMF credit and SDR allocations relative to GDP. It does not identify a
lender’s policy conditions. Missing histories, portfolio positions and outcomes remain missing.
7

Both family weights are applied to the same sample for each comparison. We require at
least 15 countries and sufficient implementation and nonimplementation observations. Unstable
or unsupported models remain in the full results registry. The finite core matrix contains 160 fits
across channels, audiences, implementation types, estimators and weights. We report country-
cluster inference and retain the Holm adjustment across all 256 attempted focal tests (Holm 1979).
The channel estimates are exploratory.
IV LIQUIDITY FINDINGS
Liquidity around implementation
Table 2 and Figure 2 report the primary comparisons. The stacked equal-family coefficient is
−0.190 (p=0.011), corresponding to 17.3% lower trading costs. The stacked market-cap coef-
ficient is−0.141 (p=0.101), corresponding to 13.1% lower trading costs. The second estimate
has a confidence interval containing zero. The explicit family averages have the same negative
direction, with different magnitudes because they give a different role to variation within each
family.
The family estimates show why the pooling target matters (Table 3). The supported auditor-
reporting comparison includes 20 adopting countries. It produces a coefficient close to zero. Public
bank reporting and environmental and social reporting have negative but imprecise estimates. The
climate comparison has a large negative coefficient, but only two adopting countries support it.
Supply-chain reporting also has a negative estimate. Thus, the pooled negative pattern does not
establish a common improvement for every disclosure family.
Interpretation and supporting comparisons
The country-condition specifications, two-year windows and other liquidity proxies are reported
in the Online Appendix. The shorter window admits IFRS as an additional supported family. This
reflects the available comparison years and company records, rather than the absence of IFRS
8

requirements from the global register. Rules introduced too late to supply the outcome window
cannot identify a liquidity effect in these data.
Event-time coefficients assess the evolution around the milestone (Online Appendix Figure
A1). Differences before implementation and sensitivity to timing, sample composition and family
weights limit a causal interpretation. Concurrent requirements present a further issue: the UK 2013
auditor and greenhouse-gas rules share treated market observations. Country clustering addresses
dependence in inference, but cannot separate the economic effects of a simultaneous bundle. We
interpret the estimates as conditional country-market comparisons around the documented require-
ments.
Confidential tax reporting is reported separately in the Online Appendix. Its point estimates
do not establish the same liquidity pattern as public disclosures. This comparison is informative
about audience and reporting purpose, but does not isolate a tax-enforcement mechanism. It is not
included in the public-disclosure headline average.
V INTERNATIONAL POLICY DIFFUSION
Diffusion Mechanism 1 – Learning from observed outcomes
Table 4 asks whether foreign-policy exposure has a different association with implementation when
earlier adopters experience better liquidity. The exposure–success interaction is 0.832 under equal
family weights and 0.907 under market-cap weights in the hazard models; the unadjustedp-values
are 0.013 and 0.002. The hazard comparison contains 202 country–family–years in 24 countries.
Because the continuous primitives are standardized, the interaction concerns their joint variation
rather than a percentage-point effect on implementation probability.
The positive hazard interaction is consistent with outcome-based learning. The within-
country interaction is not positive, however, and the hazard interaction does not survive the full
multiple-testing adjustment. Observed foreign liquidity can also reflect developments unrelated to
the policy. The evidence therefore identifies an exploratory conditional association, rather than a
demonstrated learning mechanism. Exposure to a foreign rule and observed success abroad convey
9

different information, even when neither alone provides a stable explanation of implementation.
Diffusion Mechanism 2 – Copying larger or related economies
Table 5 distinguishes following a larger economy from responding to its outcomes. The geographic
peer is fixed before the adoption period. Recent and older implementations enter separately, fol-
lowing the timing distinction in Shipan and V olden (2008); language-peer adoption provides an
additional familiarity measure. These comparisons do not show a consistent positive response
across estimators and weights. Their coverage is narrower than the full policy calendar because
the relevant peer’s history must be observed.
The legal sources nevertheless identify concrete routes for international transmission. The
Dutch standard setter’s account of expanded reporting expressly refers to developments in the
United Kingdom. National auditing requirements draw on international standards, while sustain-
ability rules use international reporting frameworks. Such source evidence establishes the avail-
ability and sometimes use of a template. It does not by itself establish that the template produced
a liquidity benefit or that a country copied it because it was successful.
Diffusion Mechanism 3 – Competition with economic rivals
The competition test uses prior implementation among countries with similar export patterns. The
independent-choice comparison has limited support. In the comparison including coordinated im-
plementations, the logit association is negative and survives the full focal-test adjustment. It does
not show the predicted positive competitive response. The corresponding within-country compari-
son is different after absorbing persistent country-family differences and shared family-year waves.
We therefore keep competition distinct from learning and from coordinated legal implementation.
Diffusion Mechanism 4 – External pressure and financial dependence
Following Koning et al. (2018), the financing test relates implementation to prior financial de-
pendence. The within-country estimates do not provide a stable positive association under both
10

weights. This proxy combines distinct financing relationships and does not identify policy con-
ditionality. The legal classification of coordinated requirements records a separate institutional
source of external influence. Joint-channel models are reported only where the intersection of the
data meets the support requirements; the registry retains failures as well as fitted comparisons.
VI CONCLUSION
We bring public-disclosure requirements into a common country-level liquidity and implementa-
tion framework. Equal-family comparisons indicate lower trading costs following implementation;
market-cap weighting produces smaller and less precise estimates. The differences across fami-
lies, timing and comparison samples prevent a conclusion that every disclosure rule generates a
common liquidity improvement.
The implementation tests distinguish the presence of foreign rules from the outcomes ob-
served among earlier adopters. A positive exposure–liquidity-success interaction appears in the
hazard models, but complementary within-country models and the full multiple-testing adjustment
do not confirm it. Together, the analyses provide a global source-linked basis for studying the mar-
ket consequences and international transmission of disclosure requirements, while leaving causal
attribution to individual rules and policymakers’ learning unresolved.
