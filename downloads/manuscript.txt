Reporting Regulation, Market Liquidity, and Policy Diffusion

Abstract
We examine reporting regulation in a global sample of 90 jurisdictions over 2000-2025. The paper has two parts. First, we aggregate stock liquidity within countries and compare changes around national implementation with changes in comparison countries. Seventeen regulation families are considered; 15 support first-year comparisons and nine support a longer window. The first-year spread estimate is -0.029 under equal family weights and +0.021 under market-cap weights, with both intervals spanning zero. Second, we examine whether competition, learning, emulation and external finance are associated with national adoption. We report both family-weighting schemes on matched samples. Only the external-finance association under market-cap family weights survives adjustment across the eight planned comparisons. The learning comparison has insufficient observations. None of the weighted associations survives the broader matrix correction. The evidence concerns national market exposure; company-specific reporting obligations are examined separately.

Introduction
Reporting rules can make companies easier to compare and reduce the information disadvantage faced by investors. They may therefore lower the cost of trading. The benefit need not be the same for every rule: some disclosures reach investors, while others are sent to tax authorities, and compliance costs or limited enforcement can offset information gains. We ask how stock-market liquidity changes around national regulation and how the policies spread across countries.

We extend the international disclosure setting in Krueger et al. (2023) beyond mandatory ESG reporting to 17 regulation families. Our first part studies market liquidity at the country level. Our second part follows Koning, Mertens and Roosenboom (2018) in examining competition, learning, emulation and external finance as explanations for adoption. Public disclosure, confidential tax reporting and regulatory institutions are kept distinct, because their information channels differ.

1. Liquidity
Datastream provides 1,055,142 stock-year observations for 99,100 vendor company records in 90 jurisdictions over 2000-2025. We form 2,050 country-year observations. The sample is broad, but is not a census of every listed company. We measure quoted bid-ask spreads, the price response to trading volume, and the share of daily returns equal to zero. Each measure requires 120 valid daily observations; higher values indicate lower liquidity. Spreads and price impact enter in logs and are winsorised by year at the first and ninety-ninth percentiles.

Table 1 describes the regulation families. For each family and adoption cohort, we hold the company sample fixed, average company liquidity changes within each country, and compare adopting countries with documented comparison countries. Already-adopting countries are excluded from the comparison group, and unknown histories are not treated as nonadoption. The first-year estimates cover 48 adopting jurisdictions across 15 families; the longer window covers 36 across nine. The first comparison is year 0 against year -1. The longer window averages years 0 to 2 against year -1, with fixed observations from -3 through +2.

We weight country averages by GDP in the year before adoption. Within each family, adoption cohorts receive weight proportional to sampled adopting-country GDP. We then combine families in two ways: each family receives the same weight, or receives weight proportional to sampled company market value before implementation. Both schemes use the same family estimates. Countries need at least 30 companies with complete outcomes, and each comparison needs three comparison countries. Standard errors allow observations within a country to move together. National rules can also arrive in common legal waves, which limits causal inference.

Table 2 asks whether liquidity improves across the regulation families. First-year log spreads are -0.029 under equal family weights and +0.021 under market-cap weights; neither is precise. Price impact and unchanged returns also depend on the weighting scheme. Only the equal-family unchanged-return result survives correction across the 12 principal liquidity comparisons. Figure 1 shows the family estimates and weights. Confidential tax country reporting receives 33.2% of market-cap weight and auditor reporting 9.9%. Figure 2 shows differences before implementation, so the subsequent changes cannot be attributed to regulation alone. The evidence does not establish a common liquidity gain across these policies.

2. Policy diffusion
We next examine why countries adopt. The adoption panel contains documented histories for 90 jurisdictions in the global market sample. We include collective implementation waves and examine independent national decisions separately. Each country remains at risk until its recorded implementation year. Countries without a documented history do not enter as nonadopters. Foreign peers can include other jurisdictions with documented histories. The market-value weights are available for the 15 liquidity-supported families; both weighting schemes use that common eligible family set, subject to mechanism-specific data availability.

Following Koning, Mertens and Roosenboom (2018), competition measures earlier adoption among countries with similar export markets. Learning compares earlier economic outcomes among neighbouring adopters and nonadopters. Emulation measures adoption among language peers. External finance measures IMF credit and aid relative to GDP. Signals precede the adoption decision. Separate logit models compare countries within the same family and year. Equal-family weights give each represented family the same total weight; market-cap weights use the same pre-implementation family values as the liquidity analysis, renormalised over the represented families. Each pair of weighted models uses identical observations and predictor units, with country-clustered uncertainty.

Table 3 reports the four mechanisms under both weights. The external-finance coefficients are negative: equal family -1.111 (adjusted p=0.142); market-cap family -0.967 (adjusted p=0.018). Only the external-finance association under market-cap family weights survives adjustment across the eight planned comparisons. The learning comparison has insufficient observations. None of the weighted associations survives the broader matrix correction. Some unweighted adoption-status models show associations, particularly for public bank reporting; the pooled weighted status models do not survive the broader correction. Adoption status describes an established regime, while timing concerns the adoption decision. The appendix reports both, along with controls, independent decisions, liquidity-based learning and combined mechanisms. These associations do not establish that one country caused another to adopt.
