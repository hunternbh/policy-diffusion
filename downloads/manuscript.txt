Reporting Regulation, Market Liquidity, and Policy Diffusion

Abstract
We study reporting regulation in a global market sample of 90 jurisdictions over 2000-2025. First, we compare country-average stock liquidity around national implementation. Seventeen policy families are considered; 13 support following-year comparisons. The spread estimate is +0.007 with equal family weights and +0.018 with market-cap weights. Both intervals include zero. Second, we examine competition, learning, emulation and external finance as explanations for policy adoption. Historical data support broader separate tests, but the joint model covers only one family. The evidence does not establish a common liquidity improvement or a general diffusion mechanism across the expanded policy set.

Introduction
Corporate reporting can help investors compare companies and trade on more equal terms. Its benefits depend on what companies disclose, who receives the information and how rules are enforced. Public sustainability reports and confidential tax returns therefore need not affect trading in the same way. We ask two questions: how does liquidity change around national regulation, and which international connections are associated with adoption?

Krueger et al. (2023) study mandatory ESG disclosure and liquidity. We extend the setting to 17 policy families, using country averages to retain international coverage. Koning, Mertens and Roosenboom (2018) study the diffusion of IFRS; we adapt their mechanisms to the expanded policy panel. These extensions change both the economic setting and the available comparisons, so agreement across policies is an empirical question.

1. Liquidity
Datastream contains 1,055,142 stock-year observations for 99,100 vendor company records in 90 jurisdictions over 2000-2025. All three liquidity measures are available for 636,960 stock-years in 86 jurisdictions. We measure bid-ask spreads, the median daily price response to trading volume, and the fraction of observed valid daily returns equal to zero. Higher values indicate less liquid trading. These records are not a census of all listed companies; each comparison uses a smaller fixed company sample.

Table 1 distinguishes the policy families and their supported cohorts. We preserve the difference between the start of a covered financial period and the arrival of public information. For UK extended audit reports, the standard was issued in 2013 but covered periods beginning from October 2012; the information-policy clock uses 2013. The broader ESG register often describes a later reporting phase. A separate benchmark uses the complete-topic mandatory dates in Krueger et al. through 2020, including comply-or-explain mandates. Neither calendar supplies actual filing dates for every company.

For each family and adoption cohort, we compare changes in country-average liquidity with changes in countries whose documented adoption comes after the outcome window. Unknown histories and already-treated countries do not supply controls. Following the outcome timing in Krueger et al., the main comparison uses liquidity in year 1 after the policy year, relative to year -1. We also average years 1-3. Companies and share lines are held fixed within a cohort; countries need 30 companies with complete outcomes and cohorts need three comparison countries. The two horizons support 13 and 10 families, respectively.

Prior-year GDP weights countries and adopting cohorts within each family. Across families, equal weights describe the average supported family; market-cap weights give more influence to families with larger sampled pre-implementation company values. These are different economic averages. They use the same family estimates, but companies can appear under several policies. The market-cap pool is not a portfolio of distinct world companies, and complete outcome requirements also determine which exposures receive weight.

Table 2 reports both weights. The following-year spread estimate is +0.007 with equal weights and +0.018 with market-cap weights; both are imprecise. Formal comparisons do not establish that the two weighted effects differ. Figure 1 shows why the point estimates move: confidential tax country reporting receives 36.1% of market-cap weight, compared with 7.7% under equal weights. Auditor reporting receives 10.8%. These exposure differences do not establish a disclosure benefit for investors.

We retain all 1,296 declared specification checks across calendars, outcomes, weights, horizons, policy groups, coordinated adoptions and controls. 837 estimates are negative and 459 positive; 40 pass the grid-wide Holm correction. The choices were declared after prior results were known. Correlated model fits are not independent confirmations, and selected significant cells do not replace the main tests. Figure 2 also shows differences before implementation on the balanced event sample. Together, these results limit a claim that the expanded policy set causes a common liquidity improvement.

2. Policy diffusion
We next ask whether countries follow foreign peers. As in Koning et al., the main outcome records whether a policy is in place; a separate sensitivity studies first-adoption timing. We include family and year effects and exclude coordinated focal adoptions from the main models. Competition measures earlier adoption among five export-market peers. Learning compares lagged GDP growth among geographic adopters and nonadopters. Emulation uses language peers as a proxy for cultural proximity. External finance measures IMF credit and aid relative to GDP.

The learning measure equals zero when no neighbour has adopted. When all have adopted, their median growth is compared with the focal country. All signals precede the focal outcome. Because many world neighbours have unknown policy histories, the main adaptation selects the ten nearest countries with documented histories. The appendix also retains the stricter world-neighbour definition, annual trade peers through their available window, legal-origin peers, domestic controls and the original 2002-2012 period. These are adaptations, not an exact replication of the JIBS country universe or cultural-distance measure.

Table 3 reports separate mechanisms on their available samples, then all four together. Separate competition and learning models cover seven and eight families; the emulation and external-finance fits are unstable and are withheld. The joint model leaves 90 observations in 15 countries, all for confidential tax country reporting. Equal and market-cap weights must then coincide. These documented phase histories are not a verified global census of adoption and nonadoption. The narrow joint sample cannot establish diffusion across the expanded panel. None of the principal associations survives adjustment for eight comparisons.
