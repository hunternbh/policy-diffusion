Disclosure Regulation, Stock Liquidity, and
International Policy Diffusion
Abstract
We examine stock liquidity around public-disclosure requirements and the international spread
of these requirements. The calendar contains 269 country–family topic milestones in 66 coun-
tries during 2000–2025, including EU climate reporting. After removing overlapping NFRD
topic tags from the pool, the three-year comparison supports 6 families, 64 implementations
and 47 countries. Equal-family stacked estimates correspond to 10.2% lower trading costs
(p=0.268); the market-cap-weighted family average corresponds to 17.1% lower trading
costs (p=0.043). The EU-inclusive climate comparison covers 14 adopting countries. We
then examine learning, copying, competition and external finance. Adoption is positively asso-
ciated with favourable growth among earlier adopters in the equal-family hazard model, while
liquidity-based learning is imprecisely estimated. The evidence indicates differences across
reporting families and pooling targets rather than a uniform market response or an established
diffusion mechanism.
Keywords:disclosure regulation; stock liquidity; policy diffusion; international reporting.
1 Introduction
Governments increasingly require companies to publish information beyond their annual finan-
cial statements. Expanded auditor reports, environmental and social reports, country reports and
supply-chain statements address different information needs. We ask two related questions. Do
these public-disclosure requirements improve stock liquidity on average? What explains their
international implementation? Figure 1 connects the source evidence to these two empirical com-
parisons.
Our primary comparison covers 64 country–family implementations in 47 countries. The
equal-family stacked estimate corresponds to 10.2% lower trading costs (p=0.268), while the ex-
plicit market-cap-weighted family average corresponds to 17.1% lower trading costs (p=0.043).
1

These targets ask different questions: an average across reporting families and an average weighted
by the market value represented in each family. Their different precision cautions against treating
all reporting requirements as having a common effect. A separate climate comparison includes EU
reporting and covers 14 adopting countries.
The diffusion tests relate implementation to information available from other countries. Fol-
lowing Koning et al. (2018), favourable growth among earlier adopters is positively associated with
implementation in the equal-family hazard model (coefficient 0.936,p=0.026). The liquidity-
learning interaction is positive but imprecise (coefficient 0.277,p=0.406). Neither compari-
son survives the full multiple-testing adjustment. We therefore distinguish a suggestive growth-
learning association from established evidence that governments learn from disclosure-induced
liquidity improvements.
Disclosure research provides the basis for these questions. Information can reduce investors’
trading costs by limiting differences in information and improving the precision and comparability
of public reports (Diamond and Verrecchia 1991, Welker 1995, Healy et al. 1999, Leuz and Verrec-
chia 2000, Healy and Palepu 2001, Beyer et al. 2010). The benefits of mandatory reporting depend
on reporting incentives, enforcement and the costs of producing and using information (Daske et al.
2008, Christensen et al. 2013, Christensen et al. 2016, Leuz and Wysocki 2016, Blankespoor et al.
2020). Thus, a requirement to report need not produce the same market response across countries
or reporting topics.
An expanding literature examines particular reporting requirements. Research on sustainability
reporting considers environmental and social information, company responses and capital-market
outcomes (Christensen et al. 2021, Krueger et al. 2024). Other studies examine expanded auditor
reports, electronic reporting, extractive payments and country-by-country tax information (Lennox
et al. 2023, Blankespoor et al. 2014, Rauter 2020, De Simone and Olbert 2022, Müller et al. 2024,
Nessa et al. 2025). These studies motivate a common comparison across reporting families. We
bring the requirements into one framework while retaining their different audiences, legal scopes
and implementation dates. The analysis asks about the average pattern across supported require-
2

ments; it does not assume that all disclosure rules are equivalent.
The second question connects capital-market research to policy diffusion. Governments can
learn from outcomes abroad, follow a visible example, respond to competitors or face external
pressure (Simmons and Elkins 2004, Simmons et al. 2006, Shipan and V olden 2008). In account-
ing, Koning et al. (2018) distinguish these explanations for international IFRS adoption. We apply
that logic to a broader disclosure calendar and separate foreign policy exposure from observed for-
eign liquidity outcomes. This distinction allows a favourable outcome abroad to enter separately
from the mere existence of a foreign rule.
Our contribution is a common country-level comparison and a source-linked implementation
calendar. The legal archive and the market-data universe are global; usable regression samples de-
pend on timing, market coverage and the availability of documented comparisons. We preserve this
distinction throughout. The paper first presents liquidity estimates and then examines international
implementation.
2 Background
2.1 What information reaches investors?
The public-disclosure families cover financial statements under IFRS, expanded auditor reports,
environmental and social reporting, climate reporting, public bank and multinational country re-
ports, extractive payments, supply chains, sustainability-related financial reporting and electronic
financial reporting. These are international families of requirements implemented through national
laws, listing rules and standards. A country name identifies the jurisdiction and timing of imple-
mentation, rather than a separate country-specific research topic.
The information differs substantially. Financial-reporting reforms change the basis on which
reported numbers are prepared. Expanded auditor reports explain matters such as significant audit
risks and key or critical audit matters. Environmental and social requirements can mandate a report
while allowing materiality judgements or explanations for particular omitted items. We therefore
3

distinguish a mandatory report from a mandate for every environmental, social and governance
metric. Optional guidance and recommended reporting frameworks do not become mandatory
simply because an exchange publishes them.
Country-by-country reporting also has distinct audiences. Public bank reports disclose spec-
ified country-level activities and financial information. Public multinational reports provide
country-level tax information under separate eligibility and publication rules. Confidential tax
reports are transmitted to tax authorities and are not, by that obligation alone, available to in-
vestors. Their liquidity consequences may reflect tax enforcement or other channels. We report
them separately from the public-disclosure headline estimate.
More useful public information can make investors less reluctant to trade against better-
informed counterparties. The expected result is lower trading costs and greater liquidity.
Preparation costs, limited enforcement, boilerplate reporting, existing information and partial
issuer coverage can weaken that response. A country-level estimate also includes firms outside the
legal perimeter and possible spillovers. It measures a market-level response to a requirement, not
an effect on every covered issuer.
Climate content cuts across reporting families. We include the environmental and climate
component of the EU Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD), which discusses greenhouse-
gas emissions and requires material environmental information from covered large public-interest
entities. This is a mandatory reporting obligation with materiality provisions, not an unconditional
mandate for every emissions metric. Earlier Danish and Greek phases are retained where docu-
mented; other NFRD cases use FY2017. France's public emissions inventories begin in 2012 and
the UK's quoted-company rule in 2013. CSRD/ESRS E1-6 subsequently specifies Scope 1, 2, 3
and total emissions, with materiality and transition reliefs; the EU first wave concerns FY2024
reports published in 2025 (European Parliament and Council 2014, European Commission 2023,
EFRAG European Lab Project Task Force 2021, European Securities and Markets Authority 2024).
These successor phases are retained in the legal inventory but lack three post years by 2025. When
one NFRD implementation is tagged under both ESG and climate, the pooled model counts that
4

country-year reform once under ESG; the separate climate estimate retains the full topic coverage.
Related studies supply institutional benchmarks. Downar et al. (2021) examine UK carbon
reporting, Joshi et al. (2020) examine public bank country reports, and She (2022) studies Califor-
nia transparency. Baik et al. (2024) studies a different US supply-chain requirement concerning
conflict minerals. Published samples do not cover identical countries, rules or years. The Online
Appendix links every family to the literature and separates historical published country lists from
the current source calendar.
2.2 Why might a reporting requirement spread?
Learning requires information about outcomes. A country exposed to an earlier adopter may be-
come more willing to implement a requirement when that adopter experiences a favourable market
outcome. Exposure alone does not establish learning: foreign requirements may be visible without
producing an observed benefit.
Copying instead concerns the visibility or legitimacy of an example. A larger nearby economy
or countries sharing a language may provide a familiar reporting template. Competition concerns
the possible cost of lagging behind an economic rival. External pressure concerns institutions or
financing relationships that influence domestic choices. Coordinated legal obligations, including
EU requirements, differ from independent national decisions. We separate those implementations
when examining the channels proposed by Koning et al. (2018).
3 Data and Research Design
3.1 Sample and datasets
The research universe contains 90 Datastream jurisdictions and market data for 2000–2025. Se-
lected source-reviewed legal provisions are available for 81 of those jurisdictions. We retain
statutes, standards, official notices, amendments and successor references, with document hashes
5

and links between the sources and coded facts. Scanned passages used in the review are checked
with OCR and page images. The archive records uncertainty rather than treating an unresolved
date or scope as nonadoption.
The public-disclosure calendar contains 269 dated country–family topic milestones in 66 coun-
tries during 2000–2025 (Table 1 and Figure 2). Twenty NFRD climate tags overlap an ESG mile-
stone and are excluded from pooled estimation under climate. The inventory records reporting
phases rather than an exhaustive census of first-ever laws. The full risk panel covers 82 countries
including confidential tax reports and 67 for public reporting. The source extension matches all
38 Krueger treatment countries and records all 32 Rauter jurisdictions; 31 of the latter belong to
our market universe. Published adoption years, legal force and first covered fiscal periods remain
distinct (Krueger et al. 2024, Rauter 2020).
We distinguish legal adoption, entry into force, the first covered financial period, an engage-
ment boundary and a statutory filing deadline. The calendar preserves the clock supported by the
source. For example, China’s initial cross-listed audit phase differs from its later broader phase;
Canada’s mandatory TSX phase differs from the earlier general standard; and Dutch expanded
reporting precedes the later EU reporting milestone. Vietnam’s greenhouse-gas addition applies
after the grandfathered 2020 annual report, whose requirements remain under the predecessor cir-
cular. The Moroccan first implementation deadline is a filing obligation, not an observed issuer
receipt. We do not invent actual filing dates or require complete national histories for an otherwise
supported implementation milestone. The evidence supplement lists the reconciled cases and their
coverage.
The market data provide three liquidity proxies following the reporting literature (Amihud
2002, Lesmond et al. 1999, Krueger et al. 2024).Bid-Ask Spreadmeasures the cost of trading im-
mediately; lower values indicate greater liquidity.Price Impactmeasures the association between
absolute returns and dollar trading volume.Zero Returnsis the fraction of observed trading days
without a price change. We use log trading costs and log price impact, screen for sufficient annual
observations, and winsorize the first two measures at the annual first and ninety-ninth percentiles.
6

An additional illiquidity index averages the three standardized measures. Higher values of each
outcome indicate less liquidity.
World Bank data provide income, GDP growth, trade, aid and IMF credit measures. IMF bilat-
eral portfolio positions measure financial exposure to foreign markets. Fixed geographic, language
and export-pattern networks identify other peer groups. These public country data complement the
licensed market observations; the firm-level Datastream files are not publicly redistributed.
3.2 Liquidity comparisons and weights
We form a separate comparison for each supported family and implementation cohort. For a mile-
stone in yearg, the primary window contains yearsg−3 throughg−1 andg+1 throughg+3. We
omit yeargas a transition year. Comparison countries have a documented later milestone after the
outcome window. An unknown date does not supply an untreated observation. This construction
avoids using already implemented cases as later-adopter controls for the same series, while recog-
nising that countries may have other or earlier reporting requirements (Goodman-Bacon 2021,
Callaway and Sant’Anna 2021, Sun and Abraham 2021, Baker et al. 2022).
We choose each company’s largest positive-market-value security line in yearg−1 and hold the
companies fixed over the window. A country must supply at least 30 qualifying companies with the
required annual market measures. This gives a comparison among firms with persistent observable
trading data and limits changes in company composition. It also reduces country coverage and can
favour larger, more actively traded companies. Two-year windows assess this restriction with a
different horizon; they do not provide an identical sample.
Within each cohort we estimate a country- and year-effect comparison, with country weights
based on GDP ing−1. Cohort weights within a family reflect the baseline GDP represented
by adopting countries. The primary sample contains 1,704 country–stack–years, 47 countries, 64
country–family implementations and 21 family–cohorts. The full climate comparison is reported
separately because the same NFRD reform also contains ESG disclosure. Country-cluster scores
retain dependence when a country appears in several cohorts or families.
7

The main presentation gives each supported family equal objective mass. We report a stacked
common-coefficient regression and an explicit average of separately estimated family coefficients.
The Online Appendix also reports market-cap family weights based on the baseline sample market
value represented by adopting-country companies. These are different economic targets, not inter-
changeable estimates. The sources and pooling rule are fixed before inspecting the revised results;
the complete coefficient registry retains both weights and every declared specification.
The secondary specifications add lagged log income per capita, GDP growth and trade/GDP;
use two-year windows; and examine alternative liquidity proxies. Event-time coefficients show
differences before and after implementation. These checks assess the comparison, but cannot
establish parallel trends or remove all coincident reforms (Roth et al. 2023, Rambachan and Roth
2023).
3.3 International implementation tests
We estimate implementation-hazard models with family and year effects, and linear probability
models with country–family and family–year effects. The latter absorb persistent country-family
differences and family-specific international waves. Independent national choices form the main
comparison; coordinated implementations enter a separate comparison. All signals use the pre-
ceding year or earlier. These models explain documented implementation timing. Because the
political decision can precede implementation, they do not establish that governments used a sig-
nal before making their original decision.
Portfolio exposure is the share of observed foreign portfolio positions in countries that have
implemented the requirement. We require documented histories to cover at least half of the ob-
served portfolio mass. The liquidity-success signal measures lower trading costs among the same
companies in earlier adopting countries. A donor must have implemented byt−2; its observed
change ends int−1. This is an observed foreign outcome, not an estimated liquidity improvement
caused by the foreign policy. We include exposure, success and their interaction together.
Following Koning et al. (2018), growth learning compares prior-year growth among earlier
8

and later adopters in a geographic network with documented histories. Copying uses a larger
geographic peer fixed with GDP in 2000, distinguishing recent and older implementation; language
peers provide a second proxy. Competition uses export-pattern peers selected in 2000. Financial
dependence adds aid to IMF credit and SDR allocations relative to GDP. It does not identify a
lender’s policy conditions. Missing histories, portfolio positions and outcomes remain missing.
Both family weights are applied to the same sample for each comparison. We require at least
15 countries and sufficient implementation and nonimplementation observations. Unstable or un-
supported models remain in the full results registry. The finite core matrix contains 160 fits across
channels, audiences, implementation types, estimators and weights. We report country-cluster in-
ference and retain the Holm adjustment across all 256 attempted focal tests (Holm 1979). The
channel estimates are exploratory.
4 Liquidity Findings
4.1 Liquidity around implementation
Table 2 and Figure 3 report the equal-family comparisons. The stacked coefficient is−0.108
(p=0.268), corresponding to 10.2% lower trading costs. The explicit family average is−0.061
(p=0.378), corresponding to 5.9% lower trading costs. Both confidence intervals include zero.
Under market-cap family weights, the stacked coefficient is−0.176 (p=0.071) and the explicit
family average is−0.187 (p=0.043), or 17.1% lower trading costs (Online Appendix Table OA6).
Weighting therefore affects the estimated average and its precision.
The six-family comparison includes expanded auditor reports, public bank reports, climate
reporting, ESG reporting, IFRS and supply-chain disclosure. Family estimates differ (Online
Appendix Table OA1). Adding documented later IFRS implementation supplies an additional
comparison country and supports a New Zealand cohort that did not meet the earlier comparison-
country requirement. That single-adopter IFRS contrast is positive, while several other family
estimates are negative. The pool should therefore be read as a weighted average across supported
9

comparisons, not evidence that each type of disclosure improves liquidity.
4.2 Climate reporting and EU coverage
The full climate comparison includes 14 adopting countries and 20 countries overall. Its trading-
cost coefficient is−0.199 (p=0.086), corresponding to 18.0% lower trading costs, with a 95%
interval from−0.429 to 0.031 in logs (Online Appendix Table OA18). Adopting countries are
Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Italy, the Netherlands, Poland,
Spain, Sweden, the UK and Vietnam. NFRD reporting is materiality-based environmental dis-
closure; France's earlier inventories and the UK and Vietnamese rules have different content and
perimeters. The estimate concerns this broad climate-reporting topic and cannot isolate a single
emissions metric.
NFRD observations already represented by an ESG milestone are omitted from the pooled cli-
mate component to avoid counting the same reform twice. They remain in the separate climate
estimate and in the complete source calendar. The pooled climate row consequently has three
adopting countries: France, the UK and Vietnam. The broad calendar contains 34 climate juris-
dictions; recent milestones lack the post years or documented comparison countries required by
the design. The supply-chain comparison retains Australia, France, the UK and the US market
observation based on California's rule, which is a subnational eligibility-based requirement rather
than a federal obligation for every issuer.
4.3 Interpretation and supporting comparisons
Country-condition specifications, shorter windows, alternative liquidity measures and event-time
estimates appear in the Online Appendix. Payments-to-governments coverage is substantially
wider after reconciliation with Rauter (2020), but this family still lacks three qualifying later-
adopter comparison countries for an eligible cohort under the fixed design. More legal coverage
therefore does not automatically produce an identified liquidity comparison. Confidential tax re-
porting remains outside the public-disclosure average.
10

Pre-implementation patterns, persistent-company selection, concurrent reforms and partial le-
gal coverage limit causal interpretation. Country clustering preserves statistical dependence across
stacks, but cannot separate simultaneous legal requirements or make a materiality-dependent report
equivalent to a universal metric mandate. We interpret the results as country-market comparisons
around documented reporting phases.
5 International Policy Diffusion
5.1 Diffusion Mechanism 1 – Learning from observed outcomes
Table 3 relates implementation to foreign-policy exposure and observed liquidity among earlier
adopters. The equal-family exposure–success interaction is 0.277 (p=0.406) in the hazard model,
using 270 country–family–years in 26 countries. The market-cap counterpart is 0.422 (p=0.179).
The within-country equal-family interaction is 0.010 (p=0.647). These estimates do not establish
that governments respond to liquidity gains abroad; the full focal-test adjusted p-values are one.
Following Koning et al. (2018), we also compare economic growth among earlier and later
adopters. The equal-family hazard coefficient is 0.936 (p=0.026), with 851 country–family–years
in 38 countries. Its market-cap counterpart is 0.912 (p=0.104); the equal-family within-country
coefficient is 0.104 (p=0.058). This positive growth comparison is suggestive of learning from
economic outcomes, but does not survive the full multiple-testing adjustment. It is different from
evidence that disclosure itself caused growth or liquidity improvements.
5.2 Diffusion Mechanism 2 – Copying larger or related economies
We distinguish following a larger economy from responding to its outcomes. Geographic peers are
fixed before the adoption period; recent and older implementations enter separately. Language-
peer adoption provides a second familiarity measure. The estimates are not precise enough to es-
tablish a consistent copying effect across the two estimators and family weights (Online Appendix
11

Tables OA2 and OA13). Legal source references to foreign standards establish the availability of
reporting templates, but do not identify policymakers' motives.
5.3 Diffusion Mechanism 3 – Competition with economic rivals
The competition test uses implementation among countries with similar export patterns in 2000.
Neither weight establishes a positive competitive response in the independent-choice models. In-
cluding coordinated European implementations changes the comparison; those estimates are re-
tained separately. An externally coordinated legal wave is not an independent national response
to rivals. Family-year effects and the independent-choice sample address different aspects of that
distinction.
5.4 Diffusion Mechanism 4 – External pressure and financial dependence
The financing test relates implementation to prior aid and IMF credit relative to GDP. The within-
country estimates do not establish a positive financing-pressure association. This aggregate proxy
combines different financing relationships and does not identify a lender's conditions. Joint-
channel models lack sufficient common support in the independent-choice sample. We retain those
failed attempts alongside the fitted models rather than filling missing histories or reporting an un-
supported joint mechanism.
6 Conclusion
We bring public-disclosure requirements into a common country-level framework, extending the
legal calendar with EU climate reporting and published country lists. The six-family liquidity
comparison gives 10.2% lower trading costs under equal-family stacking and 17.1% under the
market-cap family average, with different precision. The EU-inclusive climate comparison covers
14 adopting countries. These findings describe averages across heterogeneous reporting require-
ments and comparison samples, rather than a common benefit of every disclosure rule.
12

The implementation tests separate the presence of foreign rules from outcomes among ear-
lier adopters. A positive growth-learning association appears in the equal-family hazard model,
while the liquidity-learning interaction is imprecisely estimated. Neither establishes a diffusion
mechanism after the full testing adjustment. The source-linked calendar makes these distinctions
observable and provides a basis for more targeted issuer and policy comparisons.
