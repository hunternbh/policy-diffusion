Disclosure Regulation, Stock Liquidity, and
International Policy Diffusion
26 October 2026
Abstract
We study the capital-market consequences of public disclosure requirements and the forces as-
sociated with their international spread. We combine official policy records with global market
data and cross-border portfolio holdings. Five public-disclosure families support a common
stacked comparison covering 30 country–family implementations in 46 countries. Stock liq-
uidity improves around implementation: Bid-Ask Spread, our trading-cost proxy, is approx-
imately 20.0% lower under equal family objective mass and 18.6% lower under market-cap
objective mass in the three post-implementation years relative to the pre-reform comparison.
Explicit family averages and covariance-weighted pooling also indicate lower trading costs.
We then distinguish foreign policy exposure, observed outcomes among prior adopters, copy-
ing of larger economies and external financial dependence. The implementation-hazard models
show a positive interaction between portfolio exposure and observed foreign emissions reduc-
tions. Institutional sources document the use of international reporting templates. Together, the
analyses connect the market consequences of disclosure with the information and institutions
through which reporting requirements travel.
Keywords:disclosure regulation; stock liquidity; policy diffusion; learning; international reporting.

I INTRODUCTION
We examine stock liquidity around the introduction of public disclosure requirements and the
forces associated with their international spread. We find that stock liquidity improves around
implementation across five supported policy families. The two stacked estimates correspond to
reductions of 20.0% and 18.6% in Bid-Ask Spread, our measure of the cost of trading immediately.
Explicit equal-family and market-cap averages yield coefficients of approximately -0.199, while
covariance-weighted pooling yields -0.238. All five models use the same supported sample.
The diffusion results connect implementation to the outcomes observed abroad. The in-
teraction between foreign portfolio exposure and the share of prior adopters reporting emissions
reductions is positive under both family weights: the coefficients are 2.140 and 2.351. Exposure
has a more positive association with implementation when more prior adopters report these im-
provements. The interactions with foreign liquidity improvements are also positive, although less
precisely estimated. We also test copying, trade competition, external financial dependence and
cross-listing familiarity. In the hazard model that includes coordinated implementations, trade-
competitor adoption is negatively associated with implementation. Copying, external finance and
cross-listing show no stable positive association with implementation. These findings distinguish
the information carried by foreign experience from exposure to foreign rules alone.
Extensive literature documents how disclosure affects information asymmetry, investor par-
ticipation and the cost of trading (Diamond and Verrecchia 1991; Welker 1995; Healy et al. 1999;
Leuz and Verrecchia 2000). International reporting studies connect these benefits to enforcement
and reporting incentives (Daske et al. 2008; Christensen et al. 2013; Christensen et al. 2016). Re-
search on mandatory environmental, social and governance disclosure extends this evidence to
nonfinancial information (Krueger et al. 2024; Christensen et al. 2021). These studies provide
a foundation for understanding individual reporting reforms. There is a gap in our understand-
ing of how liquidity changes across disclosure requirements and how these requirements spread
internationally.
If disclosure rules improve transparency, reduce information asymmetry and support effi-
1

cient capital allocation, it would be reasonable to expect jurisdictions to adopt them promptly and
uniformly. In practice, the global pattern looks different. Adoption occurs at different times and
varies widely in scope. These differences raise an important question: what forces drive govern-
ments to adopt disclosure regulations? We study this question alongside the liquidity patterns that
give governments and market participants a reason to consider the rules.
We use the setting of international public-disclosure requirements because it combines a
common information channel with variation in the content, audience and timing of regulation. The
corpus identifies ten public-disclosure families, including financial reporting, auditor reporting,
ESG and climate reporting, public country reports and supply-chain disclosure. Five families sup-
port the same six-year liquidity window and documented later-adopter comparisons. This primary
sample contains 30 country–family implementations, 14 family–cohorts and 46 countries including
comparison countries. Confidential tax reporting forms a separate audience comparison.
We adjust for prior income, GDP growth and trade relative to GDP and continue to find
liquidity improvements in the control-eligible stacked sample. The Bid-Ask Spread coefficients
are -0.187 under equal family mass and -0.183 under market-cap family mass. We also distinguish
a directly stacked slope from an explicit average of policy-family coefficients. This distinction
makes clear whether a result describes a common regression slope, the typical supported family or
larger sampled market exposures.
To account for alternative explanations, we examine other liquidity measures, the timing
of the outcome window and the treatment of extreme observations. We keep the same compa-
nies within each comparison window and use countries with documented implementation after the
window as controls. We compare untrimmed annual measures with 1% and 2.5% trimming and
estimate event-time coefficients. For diffusion, we distinguish independent national choices from
coordinated implementations and include family-specific international waves in within-country
models. Following Shipan and V olden (2008) and Koning et al. (2018), the tests separate learning,
copying, competition and external pressure.
This paper contributes to several strands of literature. First, it contributes to research on
2

disclosure and capital markets by comparing different public reporting requirements within one
empirical framework. The financial-reporting literature establishes the importance of incentives
and enforcement, while nonfinancial reporting research examines information about environmen-
tal and social activities (Daske et al. 2008; Christensen et al. 2013; Krueger et al. 2024). Our
contribution is the common comparison across these requirements, using consistent market mea-
sures, company-selection rules and clearly defined pooling targets.
Second, the paper contributes to research on the international spread of accounting and fi-
nancial policies (Simmons and Elkins 2004; Simmons et al. 2006; Koning et al. 2018). When pol-
icy change is shaped by the prior choices of other jurisdictions, the process is commonly described
as policy diffusion. We distinguish broad financial exposure from observed foreign outcomes,
prominent adopters, economic competitors and external financial ties. This distinction brings the
market consequences of reporting requirements into the study of their adoption, while preserving
the separate meanings of an adoption association, a policy effect and a diffusion mechanism.
II INSTITUTIONAL SETTING AND PREDICTIONS
Public information and capital markets
Disclosure regulations have meaningful implications for investment decisions and market func-
tioning. Public reports can reduce the cost of obtaining information and make companies easier
to compare. Better information can narrow the gap between investors with different information
and support trading (Diamond and Verrecchia 1991; Healy and Palepu 2001; Beyer et al. 2010).
The channel is common to financial statements, expanded auditor reports, climate disclosures and
public country reports, although the content differs across families.
The audience matters. Confidential country-by-country tax reports are filed with tax au-
thorities and may be exchanged between them. Public bank and multinational country reports
instead make specified jurisdictional information available to investors. Confidential reports can
change behaviour through tax enforcement; public reports also change the information directly
available to the market (De Simone and Olbert 2022; Müller et al. 2024; Nessa et al. 2025). We
3

therefore separate the public-disclosure pool from confidential reporting.
National choices and international experience
When a government adopts a new policy, the impetus can come from within the polity or from out-
side it. Governments may learn from the experiences of early adopters, copy prominent templates,
respond to economic rivals or face pressure from an international institution. These mechanisms
imply different reasons for responding to the same foreign policy (Shipan and V olden 2008; Kon-
ing et al. 2018). Learning requires information about outcomes. Copying can follow the visibility
or legitimacy of the adopter even when its outcomes are not yet observed.
Cross-border financial ties can expose investors and regulators to foreign reporting prac-
tices. Cross-listing may also make firms, auditors and exchanges familiar with those practices.
This experience can lower implementation costs, but it can also reduce the additional need for a
domestic requirement when firms already comply abroad. International standards and professional
implementation networks provide another route through which reporting templates become avail-
able across countries. Coordinated legal obligations, including supranational mandates, differ from
a national decision to copy a peer.
The tension and predictions
Useful public information should reduce trading frictions. The benefits depend on whether re-
ports add information that investors can use, how much they cost to process and how reporting
requirements are implemented (Blankespoor et al. 2020, 2014; Lennox et al. 2023). We therefore
predict lower illiquidity around public-disclosure implementation and examine whether the pattern
changes across market and institutional settings.
A market benefit alone need not produce immediate adoption. Governments weigh imple-
mentation costs, domestic priorities and the credibility of foreign experience. Learning predicts a
stronger response to foreign exposure when prior adopters have visible improvements. Copying
predicts a response to a larger or related adopter beyond the outcome signal. Competition and
4

external finance provide parallel explanations for national choices. These predictions guide the
measures and comparison models; the regressions describe observable associations through which
the proposed mechanisms can be assessed.
III DATA AND EMPIRICAL DESIGN
Primary policy data
Our primary policy data are a register of official laws, standards and implementation materials.
These sources are public and verifiable. Each record identifies a country, disclosure family, au-
dience, scope and the basis for its date. Treatment measures country-market exposure to a doc-
umented rule; issuer, sector, size and jurisdictional coverage are recorded separately. The wider
register contains 482 documented country–family histories, including 60 records identifying coun-
tries outside the direct home-country geography of EU bank reporting. These geographic controls
are excluded from the implementation-hazard risk sets. The ten-family public corpus is reported
separately from confidential tax reporting and broader institutional regulation. Figure 1 shows
all dated regulatory families in the register and the country coverage of the ten public-disclosure
families. Countries are grouped by similar policy profiles, using no liquidity outcomes.
Announcement, legal application, the first covered financial period, the filing deadline and
publication can occur in different years. We use the reviewed implementation or reporting-phase
date stated in the source record. The resulting clock concerns the documented phase and does
not assume an observed filing date for every issuer. Unknown histories remain missing. The
implementation-hazard panel stops at the first recorded implementation, rather than carrying post-
implementation years into the first-event risk set.
Market and country data
The secondary market data are licensed Datastream observations during 2000–2025. The inventory
contains 1,055,142 stock-years, 99,100 vendor company records and 90 jurisdictions. For each
5

liquidity cohort, we select the company’s largest positive-market-value share line in yearg−1 and
hold the company, share line and country fixed over the full event window. At least 30 companies
with all three outcomes must remain in each country–stack. The primary stacked sample contains
1,002 country–stack–years in 46 countries (Table 1).
Following the liquidity framework in Krueger et al. (2024), we use three proxies for stock
liquidity: Bid-Ask Spread, Price Impact and Zero Return. Bid-Ask Spread is the annual median
difference between the closing ask and bid prices, divided by their midpoint. Price Impact is the
annual median absolute return divided by dollar trading volume, adapting Amihud (2002). Zero
Return is the fraction of observed valid daily returns equal to zero. The annual screening requires
120 valid observations for the relevant measure. Positive Bid-Ask Spread and Price Impact values
are trimmed within year at the 1st and 99th percentiles before logging. We also report an equally
weighted standardized illiquidity index of the three proxies. Lower values indicate greater stock
liquidity. The index uses equal weights after fixed 2000 standardization; Zero Return uses observed
valid returns as its denominator. Online Appendix Table A1 gives the complete construction and
denominators.
The IMF Portfolio Investment Positions dataset provides observed bilateral asset positions.
We select annual total portfolio assets for the total economy and total-economy counterpart, con-
vert reported scales to US dollars and exclude self positions. World Bank data provide income,
GDP growth, trade and external finance. Geographic and official-language networks define peers.
Reported UNFCCC emissions excluding land use, land-use change and forestry provide an ob-
served environmental-outcome signal. The cross-listing measure is the preceding year’s fraction
of sampled firms listed abroad. We add no interpolation or forward/backward filling to the liquidity
or emissions success measures.

IV LIQUIDITY FINDINGS
Stock liquidity around disclosure implementation
The institutional analysis identifies a common information channel across otherwise different re-
porting requirements. We first ask whether this channel is reflected in trading costs around im-
plementation. We regress country–stack–year log Bid-Ask Spread on the adopting-country post
indicator using the fixed effects and weights described above. Table 3 presents the five pooling
models on the same country and company support.
The stacked coefficient is -0.223 with equal family objective mass and -0.206 with market-
cap objective mass. These values indicate higher stock liquidity, with Bid-Ask Spread approxi-
mately 20.0% and 18.6% lower around implementation. The explicit equal-family and market-cap
averages are -0.199 and -0.199, respectively. GLS gives -0.238. Each supported family’s Bid-Ask
Spread coefficient is also negative in this common window. Figure 2, Panel B, shows the implied
reductions in trading costs and conditional intervals. All five pooling methods associate public
disclosure with higher stock liquidity in the supported sample.
Country conditions and alternative trading measures
The common sample comparison leaves open whether changing economic conditions account for
the liquidity pattern. We therefore add prior income, GDP growth and trade/GDP. The Bid-Ask
Spread coefficients remain negative at -0.187 and -0.183 on the control-complete sample (Online
Appendix Table A2). The explicit family averages are -0.147 and -0.160. This shows that the
liquidity improvement also appears after accounting for the included country conditions.
Bid-Ask Spread measures the cost of immediately trading against quoted prices. Price
Impact and Zero Return address different aspects of trading. Online Appendix Table A3 reports
all four outcomes on the same six-year sample, including the standardized index. The alternative
measures add information about how trading changes across policy families and pooling targets.

Timing, outliers and sample support
The liquidity improvements make timing and sample composition important next questions. We
therefore vary annual trimming, the first post-policy year and the length of the comparison win-

alternative. Secondary country–cohort tests examine prior liquidity, regulatory quality, market de-
velopment, income, trade and matched comparison countries (Tables A8–A9). These analyses
separate the six-year primary comparison from tests that need different data support.
The two stacked coefficients remain negative under all three trimming rules in the bench-

differ across families. Extended Appendix Table E4 then imposes minimum adopting-country
support. Taken together, these comparisons show which parts of the liquidity pattern recur across
constructions and which depend on the policy sample.
V INTERNATIONAL POLICY DIFFUSION
The liquidity analysis identifies a market pattern that could make disclosure attractive. It leaves
unanswered why countries implement requirements at different times. We study four diffusion
mechanisms: learning from observed outcomes, copying prominent or related economies, compe-
tition with economic rivals, and external pressure. Cross-listing provides a complementary test of
familiarity with foreign reporting. Each mechanism has its own measure and comparison; learning
and copying are not the only channels tested.
We first distinguish contact with foreign rules from the information carried by that contact.
Prior portfolio exposure measures the share of observed foreign assets invested in documented
adopting jurisdictions. In the implementation-hazard models, the exposure coefficients are -0.422
under equal family weights and -0.676 under market-cap weights, with standard errors of 0.803
and 0.950 (Table 4, columns (1)–(2)). The next tests ask whether outcomes observed among those
adopters change the exposure–implementation relationship.
10

Diffusion Mechanism 1 – Learning from observed outcomes
Learning means responding to evidence about how a policy works. Foreign adoption alone cannot
distinguish this response from simply following another country. Following Koning et al. (2018),
we therefore combine portfolio exposure with outcomes observed among countries that imple-
mented the requirement earlier. We use three outcome signals: stock-liquidity improvements,
emissions reductions and economic growth. The first two enter exposure-by-success interactions;
the third compares growth among geographic peers that have and have not implemented the rule.
For the market-outcome signal, success means an improvement in stock liquidity, measured
by a fall in Bid-Ask Spread for the same companies in adjacent years. We use only outcomes ob-
served after the donor country’s implementation and before the focal country’s decision. Columns
(3)–(4) of Table 4 include exposure, the observed success share and their interaction. The inter-
action coefficients are 1.835 and 2.616 under equal and market-cap family weights, with standard
errors of 1.247 and 1.446. The positive coefficients mean that exposure has a more positive asso-
ciation with implementation when more prior adopters experience liquidity improvements.
For the environmental-outcome signal, success means a fall in reported greenhouse-gas
emissions. Columns (5)–(6) yield interaction coefficients of 2.140 and 2.351, with standard errors
of 0.830 and 0.787. Both are positive and conventionally significant. A country with greater
exposure to prior adopters has a more positive exposure–implementation relationship when more
of those adopters report emissions reductions. This provides evidence of a conditional association
between observed foreign outcomes and national implementation.
The economic-growth test asks a parallel question: are countries more likely to implement
a requirement when prior adopters among their geographic peers grow faster? The measure com-
pares prior-year median growth among adopting and nonadopting peers with documented policy
histories. In the within-country models, the coefficients are -0.045 and -0.157 (Table 5, column
(1)). These estimates do not show the predicted positive relationship. The distinction matters be-
cause a visible policy-specific outcome and overall economic growth convey different information
to a government considering a reporting rule.

Diffusion Mechanism 2 – Copying larger or related economies
Countries may follow a prominent adopter because its policy is visible or considered legitimate.
This mechanism need not depend on favourable outcomes. We test copying with two measures:
prior implementation by a fixed larger geographic peer and the fraction of language peers that have
already implemented the requirement. The larger peer is chosen using GDP in 2000; its identity
does not change as policies are adopted. We separate implementation in the preceding year from
older implementation, following the timing logic in Shipan and V olden (2008).
The independent-choice larger-peer sample contains 12 countries, below the stated 15-
country support requirement. Online Appendix Table A10 reports this coverage and the all-
documented comparison. In the latter, recent-peer coefficients are 0.118 and -0.742; older-peer
coefficients are -0.917 and -1.905. The language-peer coefficients in the within-country models
are 0.058 and 0.043 (Table 5, column (2)). Together, the estimates do not show a stable positive
response to implementation by a larger or linguistically related country.
Official sources nevertheless show that international templates are available to national
standard setters. The XRB describes New Zealand Climate Standards as based on TCFD, while
IAASB and IFAC materials document international auditor-reporting standards and their dissem-
ination (Table 6). These cases identify practical routes through which reporting practices travel.
They complement the adoption comparisons by showing the institutional infrastructure through
which a country can obtain a reporting template.
12

Diffusion Mechanism 3 – Competition with economic rivals
Copying concerns the prominence or proximity of an adopter. Competition instead concerns the
economic consequences of falling behind a rival. We identify five countries with the most similar
export-destination patterns in 2000 and measure prior adoption among those with documented
histories. Fixing the network before the adoption period prevents the choice of competitors from
responding to subsequent implementation.
The independent-choice sample has nine countries and does not meet the support require-
ment. With all documented implementations, the logit coefficients are -1.292 under equal weights
and -1.834 under market-cap weights, with standard errors of 0.229 and 0.314. Both remain signif-
icant after the diffusion-matrix adjustment. These estimates associate greater competitor adoption
with lower implementation likelihood in that sample. The corresponding within-country coeffi-
cients are near zero after absorbing country–family differences and family-specific international
waves. This pattern makes the distinction between independent national decisions and coordinated
legal requirements central to the interpretation of competition.
Diffusion Mechanism 4 – External pressure and financial dependence
Domestic reporting choices may also be shaped by external institutions. Following Koning et al.
(2018), we examine a country’s reliance on external finance. The measure adds net official devel-
opment assistance to the World Bank series for IMF credit and SDR allocations, scales the total
by GDP, and uses the preceding year’s value. It measures financial dependence; the data do not
identify a lender’s policy conditions.
The within-country coefficients are -0.002 under equal family weights and -0.067 under
market-cap weights (Table 5, column (3)). The standard errors are 0.015 and 0.050. These esti-
mates provide no positive association between greater measured financial dependence and imple-
mentation. We also distinguish independent national choices from coordinated legal obligations
in the policy register. That distinction records an institutional source of external pressure directly,
rather than assigning it from the financing proxy.
13

Additional channel – Cross-listing and familiarity with foreign reporting
Cross-listing offers a separate route through which firms, auditors and regulators become familiar
with foreign reporting practices. Such familiarity may lower the cost of a domestic requirement.
It may also reduce the additional need for a local rule when firms already report abroad. We
measure the preceding year’s share of sampled firms that are cross-listed and estimate the same
within-country model.
The coefficients are 0.0002 and 0.0022 under equal and market-cap family weights, with
standard errors of 0.0170 and 0.0175 (Table 5, column (4)). The sample contains 870 risk-year
observations in 25 countries and nine public-disclosure families. These estimates show little asso-
ciation between general cross-listing prevalence and national implementation. Because the mea-
sure does not identify each issuer’s host-country reporting rule, it captures familiarity with foreign
reporting generally. The result separates this broad exposure channel from the outcome-based
learning tests.
VI CONCLUSION
We study public-disclosure requirements in two parts: their liquidity patterns and their interna-
tional implementation. The five retained pooling models all produce negative Bid-Ask Spread
coefficients on the same supported sample. The two directly stacked estimates imply higher stock
liquidity, with Bid-Ask Spread approximately 20.0% and 18.6% lower around implementation un-
der the stated family objective masses. The diffusion analysis separates exposure to foreign rules
from observed outcomes among prior adopters and the availability of reporting templates. It shows
a positive exposure–emissions-reduction interaction in the implementation-hazard specifications.
The evidence also identifies the conditions that matter for interpretation. Differences in the
trading-cost proxy are present before implementation in the event analysis. Two supported liquid-
ity families have only one adopting country, and GLS assigns 59.6% of its benchmark weight to
climate disclosure. The hazard interaction does not survive the full diffusion-matrix adjustment,
and complementary within-country specifications give different estimates. We address these issues
14

by holding companies fixed, using documented comparison histories, reporting event leads, vary-
ing timing and weights, and retaining support restrictions and complete model matrices. Future
work can sharpen causal interpretation with more complete earliest-law histories, observed issuer
filing dates and direct evidence of policymakers’ use of foreign outcomes.
