Reporting Regulation, Stock Liquidity, and International Diffusion

Abstract
We examine reporting regulation across countries using stock-market data from 90 jurisdictions over 2000-2025. Seventeen policy families are considered; 15 support first-year liquidity comparisons and nine support a balanced longer window. We estimate changes around recorded national implementation dates relative to comparison jurisdictions, then aggregate across regulation families. First-year log-spread estimates are -0.029 with equal family weights and +0.021 with market-capitalisation weights. The market-cap estimate is imprecise, and the liquidity measures do not establish a uniform improvement. We also examine international diffusion using lagged competition, learning, emulation and external-finance measures. No association survives correction across the complete model matrix. The analysis describes exposure to national policy changes; it does not establish the reporting obligations of every company.

1. Introduction
Disclosure regulation can give investors more information and make companies easier to compare. If it narrows information differences between traders, shares may become easier to trade. Governments have extended reporting beyond financial statements to nonfinancial performance, ownership, supply chains and payments to governments. We ask whether liquidity changes accompany this wider set of reforms and how the policies spread across countries.

We extend the international disclosure setting in Krueger et al. (2023) to a wider set of regulation families. National implementation dates are linked to company stock outcomes to examine liquidity before and after implementation. We then examine international adoption using the diffusion framework of Koning, Mertens and Roosenboom (2018). Original company reports provide supplementary evidence on reporting scope and timing.

The policies differ in what information they provide and who receives it. Public country reports can inform investors; confidential country-by-country tax reports go to tax authorities. Audit institutions, governance revisions and sectoral rules may affect markets without creating a new disclosure duty for every listed company. An average across these policies describes a broad policy environment rather than one common reporting treatment.

2. Sample and research design
Datastream supplies 1,055,142 stock-year observations in 90 jurisdictions from 2000 to 2025, linked to 99,100 vendor company records. One stock-year is one stock observed for one year. Each liquidity measure requires at least 120 valid daily observations. We use the log relative quoted spread, log Amihud price impact and fraction of observed returns equal to zero; higher values indicate lower liquidity. Logged measures are winsorised each year at the first and ninety-ninth percentiles.

Table 1 reports all 17 attempted families. The first-year comparisons cover 30,703 distinct treated company records in 48 jurisdictions across 15 families. The longer-window comparisons cover 18,470 treated company records in 36 jurisdictions across nine families. Companies may appear under several policies; company-policy exposures are not unique companies. Some families have broad international support, while others have only one supported treated jurisdiction.

For each regulation family and implementation cohort, we compare the change from the preceding year in implementing jurisdictions with the change in comparison jurisdictions. Already implementing countries do not serve as controls for that family. Comparisons use documented later dates; the bank home-jurisdiction contrast also includes explicitly coded non-EU jurisdictions, which may still have national rules or affected subsidiaries. Missing histories are not coded as nonadoption. National implementation is an exposure assignment, not proof that each company must report.

We select one share line per company using the largest market value before implementation and keep it fixed within each cohort. The first-year design uses years -1 and 0. The longer window requires complete outcomes from -3 through +2 and averages years 0-2 relative to -1. Each country requires 30 complete companies; each comparison requires 30 treated companies, 30 comparison companies and three comparison jurisdictions. The narrower longer-window sample is not a continuation of the first-year aggregate.

Within a family, prior-year country GDP is divided across its complete company sample. Across families, equal weights describe the average supported family; market-cap weights use the sampled treated companies' pre-implementation market value. These are exposure weights across country-policy cohorts, not shares of worldwide legally covered issuers. Company exposure can recur across families. Country-clustered intervals condition on the observed implementation calendar; collective legal waves and few treated countries can make policy-level uncertainty larger. Date-plus-one and independent-national-choice specifications remain in the supplement.

3. Liquidity
Table 2 reports the international estimates under both family weights. First-year log spreads are -0.029 under equal weights and +0.021 under market-cap weights. Equal-weight price impact is -0.045 and the unchanged-return fraction is -0.0082; the corresponding market-cap estimates are +0.019 and -0.0027. Only the equal-weight unchanged-return result survives the adjustment across the 12 principal liquidity comparisons. The evidence does not establish lower trading costs across regulation families.

Figure 1 reports the family estimates and their weights. Confidential country-by-country tax reporting receives 33.2% of first-year market-cap weight; international auditor reporting receives 9.9%. These weights reflect the global sampled exposure population. Figure 2 reports the balanced nine-family profiles. Pre-implementation differences are material, so subsequent changes cannot be attributed to the policies alone. Overlapping reforms, sector-specific eligibility and prior voluntary disclosures remain alternative explanations.

4. International diffusion
We examine whether implementation follows earlier adoption among trade peers, earlier economic outcomes among geographic neighbours, language or institutional peers, and external financial dependence. Signals precede the focal decision. Table 3 reports four pooled timing regressions with family-year strata and country-clustered uncertainty. The samples differ because the required histories differ. The external-finance coefficient is negative in this narrow comparison; its adjusted p-value is 0.0013 across these four models but 0.142 across the complete stable-coefficient matrix.

The full exercise contains 1,330 timing and adoption-status designs and 433 stable focal coefficients. None survives the full multiplicity correction. These associations do not establish that one country caused another to adopt. Together, the liquidity and diffusion analyses provide international evidence on recorded policy implementation while distinguishing policy exposure, company eligibility and the information actually available to investors.
