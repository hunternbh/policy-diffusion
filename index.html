DISCLOSURE REGULATION · CAPITAL MARKETS

Reporting rules and
the cost of trading

Do reporting requirements make shares easier to trade? We examine several kinds of financial and nonfinancial disclosure, then ask how these rules spread across countries.

The figures and tables below use the paper’s results. Each is followed by a short explanation.

01 / THE OVERALL COMPARISON

What this tells us

In the first report year, the market-cap-weighted comparison shows wider quoted spreads and greater price impact. In the longer window, spreads are lower, while price impact remains higher. The measures do not establish a single, common improvement in liquidity.

The bid–ask spread is the gap between quoted buying and selling prices. Price impact measures how much prices move with trading. The third measure is the share of observed returns that are unchanged. Higher values indicate lower measured liquidity.

02 / DIFFERENCES BETWEEN REPORTING RULES

Why the mix of rules matters

Auditor reporting receives 87.6% of the first-year market-cap weight. The overall estimate therefore says much more about this reporting regime than about the smaller issuer studies of environmental, governance or government-payment disclosures.

Giving each family the same weight changes the first-year spread estimate from +0.190 to −0.078. This is a material difference in what the average represents. See the complete weighting comparison.

03 / BEFORE AND AFTER REPORTING

Why timing matters

The reporting issuers and their comparison firms already differ before the first required report. That makes it difficult to attribute later changes entirely to the new rule. Earlier voluntary disclosures, industry conditions and other reforms can also affect trading.

These profiles cover seven reporting families. They should not be joined to the nine-family first-year comparison as though all observations came from one unchanged sample.

04 / HOW REPORTING RULES SPREAD

What the tests support

Across 1,330 model designs, 433 coefficients have stable estimates. None survives the adjustment for the full number of tests. That adjustment helps distinguish a recurring relationship from a result that could appear simply because many comparisons were made.

We therefore cannot identify a confirmed diffusion mechanism in this panel. The complete results retain the estimated relationships, models without sufficient data and unstable estimates.

How to read the evidence

Each reporting study compares changes for reporting issuers with changes for its comparison group. Country GDP weights are used within studies; the market value of treated issuers before reporting determines weights across families. The market panel contains 1,055,142 security-years in 90 jurisdictions. Verified issuer coverage supports nine first-year families and seven longer-window families, rather than every issuer under all 17 policy families.

What counts as ESG, climate or country-by-country reporting?

Mandatory and voluntary reporting. A reporting requirement is distinguished from a company’s earlier voluntary disclosure. A first statutory report need not be the first time investors received the information.

Environmental, social and governance disclosure. A rule covering greenhouse-gas emissions is environmental disclosure; it is not automatically a requirement to report across all three ESG categories. The UK carbon study and German statutory nonfinancial-reporting study are therefore kept distinct.

Public and confidential country-by-country reporting. Public bank reports make country information available to investors. Confidential tax reports go to tax authorities and are not public issuer disclosures. Their filing dates cannot be inferred from the date a law was enacted.

Historical group boundaries, financial thresholds, listing venues and some earliest publication dates remain incomplete. No missing filing date or unsupported policy effect is filled with an assumed value.

Abstract and full manuscript

We study how reporting mandates relate to stock liquidity across financial and nonfinancial disclosure regimes. Our setting combines a global market panel with original reports that establish coverage and reporting dates for nine regulation families. We distinguish the average family from the regulatory exposure of large issuers. First-year log-spread comparisons are -0.078 under equal family weights and +0.190 under market-capitalisation weights. Audit reporting accounts for 87.6% of the latter weight; a family-by-family decomposition explains the difference between the aggregates. Seven families support longer-window comparisons. Earlier disclosure, different liquidity measures and pre-policy trends limit a common causal interpretation. We also examine international adoption using lagged competition, learning, emulation and external-dependence measures. No coefficient survives the full multiplicity correction. The evidence highlights policy composition and the information already available to investors when assessing reporting regulation.
