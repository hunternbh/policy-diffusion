Periods differ

Hong Kong general E/S disclosures: FY starts 2016. Singapore: FY ends from 31 December 2017. Ireland: FY starts from 1 August 2017. Fiscal boundaries are not observed public filing dates.

Scope differs

India’s initial top 100 differs from a later top 500 expansion. Argentina’s cited law is subnational. Canada, Pakistan and China are withheld from the full-topic class under the stated evidence definition.

A shorter paper.

We study how reporting mandates relate to stock liquidity across financial and nonfinancial disclosure regimes. Our setting combines a global market panel with original reports that establish coverage and reporting dates for nine regulation families. We distinguish the average family from the regulatory exposure of large issuers. First-year log-spread comparisons are -0.075 under equal family weights and +0.184 under market-capitalisation weights. Audit reporting accounts for 84.3% of the latter weight; a family-by-family decomposition explains the difference between the aggregates. Seven families support longer-window comparisons. Earlier disclosure, different liquidity measures and pre-policy trends limit a common causal interpretation. We also examine international adoption using lagged competition, learning, emulation and external-dependence measures. No coefficient survives the full multiplicity correction. The evidence highlights policy composition and the information already available to investors when assessing reporting regulation.

1. Introduction

Investors need information to assess the prospects and risks of the firms they trade. Reporting mandates can make that information more widely available and easier to compare. Governments have extended these requirements well beyond financial statements to environmental performance, governance, supply chains and payments to governments. For accounting research, this creates a natural question: do the liquidity benefits associated with one reporting regime extend to a broader set of mandates?

The answer is not clear in advance. A requirement can reduce information differences between traders, but its contribution depends on what firms already report, who receives the disclosure and which firms must respond. A confidential tax return supplies information to the authorities without necessarily informing investors. A new governance code can require a response even where the underlying practice is already common. Mandatory reporting therefore need not mark the first arrival of information in the market.

We follow Krueger et al. (2023) in linking reporting rules to stock liquidity and then examining why the aggregate differs across settings. Liquidity matters to investors through trading costs and provides a common outcome for financial and nonfinancial disclosure. Our extension treats the mix of regulation as part of the research question. Equal family weights describe the average supported reporting regime; market-capitalisation weights give greater influence to regimes covering larger sampled issuers. Neither weighting scheme is selected for the direction of its result.

The main finding concerns this distinction. The two first-year spread comparisons differ by 0.259 log points while holding the family estimates fixed. The market-capitalisation aggregate is dominated by auditor reporting, with an effective family count of 1.40 rather than nine. This explains why an average across types of regulation can give a different answer from an average weighted toward large issuers. We complement the capital-market analysis with the diffusion framework of Koning, Mertens and Roosenboom (2018).

2. Sample and research design

The market panel contains 1,055,142 security-years in 90 jurisdictions from 2000 to 2025. We measure liquidity using the log relative quoted spread, log Amihud price impact and the fraction of observed returns equal to zero. Each proxy requires 120 valid observations; logged proxies are winsorised annually at the first and ninety-ninth percentiles. Higher values indicate lower liquidity. Historical identifiers link the selected reporting parents to ordinary shares; vendor quote identifiers do not by themselves establish legal coverage.

Table 1 describes nine families for which issuer reports or filing evidence support a comparison. The UK carbon study uses four original FY2013 annual reports recovered from the all-data archive. The 2013 law requires quoted-company greenhouse-gas disclosure; this is distinct from comprehensive E/S/G reporting and later climate-risk rules. Prior annual reports document information already available. For Fresnillo, the official registry identifies the FY2015 filing on 16 June 2016, separately from the public report on 15 June. Approval dates and archive stamps are never substituted for publication.

For each family and reporting cohort, we compare the change in treated issuers with the change in comparison issuers relative to year g-1. Calendar-based comparisons use known later implementers, with thirty issuers per country and three comparison markets. Report-based comparisons use documented reporting obligations and period-specific comparison evidence, requiring three treated and twenty comparison issuers. The first-year design uses g-1 and g. The longer-window design fixes support from g-3 through g+2 and averages years 0-2; it covers seven families.

Within each study, prior-year GDP is allocated across the fixed country issuer universe, and cohort estimates are combined using treated mass. Across families, we report both equal weights and prior-year treated-issuer market capitalisation. All three outcomes use the same supported families; banking enters once. Source-selected US comparisons do not certify every foreign subsidiary or venue exemption. Repeated issuer-policy exposures are not a unique-firm investment portfolio. Sparse common policy waves, cross-country and industry differences, and earlier disclosure constrain causal inference.

3. Liquidity and policy composition

Table 2 reports both aggregates. Across nine report-based families, first-year spreads are -0.075 under equal weights and +0.184 under market-capitalisation weights. Figure 1 shows the underlying comparisons and economic weights. Audit reporting receives 84.3% of market-capitalisation weight, compared with 11.1% under equal weights. The decomposition attributes the entire aggregate difference to these observed weight changes; it does not change any family coefficient.

Among the seven longer-window families, spread comparisons are -0.060 and -0.079. Price-impact comparisons are +0.071 and +0.158. Figure 2 shows the pre-policy differences as well as subsequent outcomes. These measures do not support a uniform improvement in trading conditions. The narrower longer-window sample also prevents interpreting the two horizons as one event path. The broader geographic panel in Table 2 is a separate exposure comparison, because country assignment alone does not establish issuer eligibility.

4. International diffusion

Following Koning et al., we ask whether adoption is associated with earlier implementation among trade peers, prior outcomes in neighbouring countries, cultural and institutional peers, or external financial dependence. All signals precede the focal decision. The corrected UK carbon date is incorporated into the adoption-risk panel and the lagged peer measures. Across 1,330 timing and status designs, 433 focal coefficients have stable estimates and 0 survive the full Holm correction. These exploratory associations do not establish a diffusion mechanism. Taken together, the analyses show why the covered population, policy mix and prior information must accompany any claim about the capital-market consequences of reporting mandates.
