Sample and datasets

We study 2000–2025 using market data from 90 countries. The public-disclosure calendar contains 211 country–family implementations in 65 countries, across ten reporting families. The source archive also retains amendments, successor rules and their coverage.

1. Liquidity

Under equal family weights, the stacked estimate indicates 17.3% lower trading costs after implementation (p = 0.011). Market-cap weights give a smaller estimate of 13.1% (p = 0.101); its confidence interval includes zero. Lower trading costs indicate greater stock liquidity.

Responses differ across families. Auditor reporting now has 20 adopting countries in the main comparison; climate disclosure has only two. Country weights use pre-implementation GDP. Family weights are equal or based on the adopting-country sample’s baseline market value. The paper reports both a stacked regression and an explicit average of family effects.

The estimates describe country-market comparisons around documented implementation milestones. They do not establish the effect on every legally covered issuer. Pre-implementation patterns, concurrent reforms and incomplete coverage limit causal interpretation.

2. Diffusion

Following the JIBS framework, we distinguish learning, copying, competition and external financial dependence. The learning test asks whether foreign-policy exposure has a more positive relation with implementation when earlier adopters experience lower trading costs.

The hazard interaction is positive under both weights. The within-country comparison and the adjustment across the full test matrix do not confirm it. This is preliminary evidence of a conditional association, rather than a demonstrated learning mechanism.

Copying, competition and external-finance comparisons are reported in the paper and full results. In the comparison including coordinated requirements, competitor adoption is negatively associated with implementation in the hazard model; the corresponding within-country comparison differs.

Implementation dates are not always political decision dates. Foreign liquidity changes are observed outcomes, not estimated gains caused by the foreign rule. Samples vary with portfolio, outcome and peer-history coverage.
